Who this book is for

The book assumes you can write a program and have used a database, and nothing beyond that. It does not assume you have run software for a large number of users. Each chapter starts from the problem that makes a technique necessary, because a technique learned without its problem is hard to apply and easy to misuse.

It covers two kinds of design that are usually taught apart:

  • High-level design decides which machines and services exist, what data lives where, and how requests move between them. Parts I to VIII and Part XI cover it.
  • Low-level design decides the classes, interfaces and data structures inside one of those services. Parts IX and X cover it, with code in TypeScript.

The two meet constantly. A rate limiter is a box on an architecture diagram and also a small class with a tricky concurrency problem, and the book treats it as both.

Reading order

Part I is written to be read in order, and every later part leans on it. After that the parts are mostly independent. If you are preparing for interviews, read Part I, then the method chapter at the start of each case-study part, then work backward from the case studies to the concept chapters they cite. If you are designing something at work, the system map is the quickest way to find the chapter for the component you are thinking about.

Chapters that are listed but not yet published are marked as such in the table of contents. References to them appear as plain text until they exist.

Conventions

Color in diagrams

Four colors keep the same meaning in every diagram. Line style repeats the meaning, so the diagrams still read in grayscale or with a color vision deficiency.

  • The synchronous request path: a caller is waiting
  • Data at rest and the reads and writes that touch it
  • Asynchronous work: queued now, done later (always dashed)
  • A failure, or the path taken because of one
  • Everything else

Side notes

Side knowledge sits in a shaded box with a title of its own. A side note is always skippable: history, a naming quirk, or a pointer to where an idea is used in another field.

Callouts

Common mistake. An error that is easy to make and that the chapter has just given you the means to avoid.

In an interview. How the idea tends to be tested, and what a strong answer includes.

In production. What changes when the system is real: operations, cost, and the failure nobody drew on the whiteboard.

Figures you can play

Figures are numbered by chapter. A figure that shows a sequence, such as a handshake or a failover, has a row of controls under it. Play builds the drawing up one step at a time and explains each step in a sentence. What is sent travels along its arrow under its own name, such as GET photo:871, and where the story is about data, a row under the sentence shows what each part holds after the step, with the values that just changed picked out. The arrows move one step yourself, the slider jumps to any step, and the speed button changes the pace. Some figures tell more than one story on the same drawing, for example a read and a write, and have a button for each. Nothing plays until you ask it to.

With scripts turned off, or on paper, the same figure shows the finished drawing with its steps as a numbered list, so nothing is lost. Enlarge under any figure opens it at full size, which helps on a phone. On charts, pointing at or tapping a value shows the numbers behind it.

Calculators

Calculators recompute as you type or drag a slider, and explain the result in a sentence. The numbers they start with are the ones used in the text around them. Where the result has a shape, such as a queue draining, a budget running out or keys moving on a hash ring, the calculator draws it and redraws as you change the numbers.

Cross-references and questions

A box titled Connects to lists where the same idea reappears elsewhere in the book, and pointing at a link to another chapter shows what that chapter covers. Each chapter ends with its main points, a few questions with answers you can reveal, and its sources. After revealing an answer you can mark it as known or ask to see it again later. The contents page keeps count of the ones to come back to.

Using the site

  • Search. Press Ctrl K or /, or use the search button. It finds chapters, sections and every term the book defines. The glossary lists the same terms from A to Z.
  • On this page. Beside each chapter is a list of its sections drawn as a timeline: each bar is as long as the section takes to read, and the bars fill in as you go. On a small screen the same list opens from the bar at the bottom.
  • Progress. Reaching the end of a chapter marks it as read, and the button at the top or bottom of the chapter changes that. The contents page shows where you stopped.
  • Highlights and notes. Select any text in a chapter and choose Highlight or Add a note. With a keyboard, Alt H and Alt N do the same for the selected text. Select a highlight to change its note or remove it. Notes and highlights lists everything you have marked, links each one back to its place, and can save it all as a Markdown file or as a backup you can restore in another browser.
  • Theme. The button at the top right switches between a light and a dark theme.

What the site remembers (the theme, the chapters you have read, where you stopped, your answers to the questions, and your highlights and notes) is kept in this browser only. Nothing is sent anywhere, and clearing the site’s data resets it. A different browser or device starts fresh, which is why the notes page offers a backup file. If a chapter is revised, a highlight follows its sentence to the new place. If the sentence itself was rewritten, the highlight is kept on the notes page and marked as detached.

How far to trust a number

Most of system design is stable. The way a B-tree works, or the reason two replicas can disagree, has not changed in decades. Those explanations are written from first principles and should stay correct.

Some facts do change: the share of traffic on a protocol, the limits of a cloud service, the largest machine you can rent. Each of those is given with a date and a source, and each chapter header states when its facts were last checked. Treat a dated number as a snapshot and follow the source for the current value.

Capacity figures such as “a server handles a few thousand requests per second” are orders of magnitude for planning. They are not benchmarks. Before you commit to a design, measure your own workload on your own hardware.
