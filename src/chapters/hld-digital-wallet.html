The problem

The problem. Design the back end of a digital wallet. Users hold a balance in an app, top it up from a bank, send money to each other and pay merchants. At the peak the system handles a million transfers a second. Money must never be created or lost, a transfer happens completely or not at all, and for any wallet the company must be able to show its balance at any past moment and the history that produced it.

A digital wallet is an account of stored money held by an app on a user’s behalf, whose balance changes by transfers recorded by the app itself rather than by a bank. The payment chapter () kept its ledger in one database, because a hundred payments a second fit. A million transfers a second do not fit any single machine’s database, so wallets are split into partitions, and the hard part becomes a transfer between two wallets in different partitions, which no single local transaction can cover. In the terms of , this is the second money problem: transfers across machines at volume, with reproducible balances.

The first line is why the design is not a sharded relational database (). The chapter ran a transfer, two locked updates and two ledger lines, through pgbench on PostgreSQL 16: about 5,000 a second on a small two-core server, conserving every cent. Bigger servers do more, but a million a second would still take dozens of nodes, and most transfers would cross between them. The design instead keeps each partition’s balances in memory and records changes in a log, which a single thread can process far faster, as Step 6c measures. The cost the estimate also shows: splitting more finely makes more transfers cross partitions, and a crossing transfer costs three events instead of one.

The transfer id, made once by the app, is the idempotency key, and the partitions remember it, so a retry returns the first outcome instead of moving money again (). A transfer within one partition completes in the call; one across partitions is accepted with the sender’s money already taken and in flight, and completes a moment later. Every read says which event it reflects, because reads come from copies that follow the log.

The log is the record and everything else is derived from it, which is event sourcing () with the read side split off as in CQRS (). Wallets are assigned to partitions by a hash of the wallet id, with a directory for wallets that must move, such as a merchant that outgrows its share ().

Step 5: The design

Commands. A stateless command service routes each transfer to the partition that holds the sending wallet. A partition is three servers running a consensus protocol over one log: the leader checks each command against the balances in its memory, appends the resulting events, and applies them once a majority of the servers have stored them, the state machine replication of . If the leader fails, a follower with the same log and the same state takes over.

Across partitions. A transfer whose receiver lives elsewhere is finished by a relay that reads the sender’s log, delivers the second half to the receiver’s partition, and reports back. Step 6a is about why this is safe.

Reads. Balances for display, history and reports are read models fed from the events, never from the partitions, so reads cannot slow the log down.

Step 6a: Transfers between partitions

A transfer from Ana in one partition to Ben in another changes two logs on different machines. lists the ways to do that: two-phase commit, try-confirm-cancel and sagas. The obvious way, which is none of them, comes first, because it is what code does by default: take the money from one side, then give it to the other.

The chapter moved 100,000 transfers between four partitions while the process doing it crashed at random, one step in a thousand, then one in a hundred. Debiting and then crediting, with nothing recording the transfer between the two writes, lost 87 transfers and 2,303 euros at the lower rate, and 963 transfers and 25,131 euros at the higher one: money taken from senders that never reached anyone. As a saga, the debit and the record of the transfer are one event in the sender’s log, so a crash at any later point leaves something to resume from. The relay delivered again after each crash; receivers recognized 95 and 1,041 repeated deliveries by their transfer id and ignored them; transfers to closed accounts were returned to the sender, 92 and 84 of them. Money was conserved in every run.

The three textbook protocols differ in what the rest of the system waits for while a transfer is unfinished. The test sent 100,000 transfers at a thousand a second, a tenth of them to one merchant’s wallet, and let the coordinator crash in the middle of one transfer in a thousand, taking five seconds to recover.

The hot merchant decides it. A wallet that receives a hundred transfers a second cannot be locked for the length of a network round trip, let alone a coordinator’s recovery. The saga keeps every step a local, append-only event in one partition and accepts that, for a moment, money has left one wallet and not yet reached another. The design makes that state explicit: the sender’s partition holds it as in flight, the sum of balances plus money in flight never changes, and the API reports the transfer as in flight until it settles.

Step 6b: Balances as a fold over events

Each partition is event-sourced: it stores what happened, and the balances are computed from it. Two pure functions hold all the rules. decide checks a command against the current state and returns events; apply changes the state for one event, and is the only code that does.

export function apply(s: State, e: Event): State {
  const add = (a: string, n: number) => s.balances.set(a, (s.balances.get(a) ?? 0) + n);
  switch (e.type) {
    case "Opened": s.balances.set(e.account, 0); break;
    case "Deposited": add(e.account, e.amount); s.seen.add(e.ref); break;
    case "Transferred": add(e.from, -e.amount); add(e.to, e.amount); s.seen.add(e.id); break;
    case "SendStarted": add(e.from, -e.amount); s.outgoing.set(e.id, { from: e.from, amount: e.amount }); s.seen.add(e.id); break;
    case "Received": add(e.to, e.amount); s.seen.add(e.id); break;
    case "ReceiveRefused": s.seen.add(e.id); break;
    case "SendCompleted": s.outgoing.delete(e.id); break;
    case "SendReturned": { const o = s.outgoing.get(e.id); if (o) add(o.from, o.amount); s.outgoing.delete(e.id); break; }
    case "Rejected": s.seen.add(e.id); break;
  }
  s.version += 1;
  return s;
}

The test drove one partition with 20,000 wallets through 200,000 transfer requests, of which 16,299 were refused for lack of funds and 3,967 were retries that decided nothing new. Replaying the 240,000 events from an empty state gave a state with the same hash as the live one, and so did loading a snapshot taken at event 216,000 and replaying the rest. For one wallet with 21 events, its balance after each event equaled the sum of the events so far: the history explains every balance. The same holds at any moment, so “what was Ana’s balance at midnight on 30 September” is a replay up to that time.

This only works if apply is deterministic: the same events must produce the same state on every server and in every replay, years later. The test broke that on purpose with an apply that waived a one-cent fee in odd seconds of the clock. Two replicas applying the same events, half a second apart, ended with different states. Anything that varies, the time, a random choice, an exchange rate, is decided once in decide and written into the event, where every replay finds the same value ().

Snapshots bound how much replay a restart needs. In one run on this chapter’s machine, replaying 1.2 million events took 1.1 s, and loading a snapshot and replaying the last 100,000 took 0.14 s. A partition saves a snapshot every few minutes; a new follower or a restarted leader loads the latest and replays the log from there.

Step 6c: A million transfers a second

Inside one partition, everything happens on one thread in memory: no locks, because nothing else touches the balances, and no disk reads. The chapter’s TypeScript partition decided and applied about 517,000 transfers a second on one thread in one run. Martin Fowler described the same design at LMAX in 2011: a business logic processor that runs in memory, event-sourced, handling 6 million orders a second on a single thread.

The limit is making each event durable before it counts. Writing every event to disk and waiting for the flush caps a log at the disk’s flush rate. Group commit () writes all the events that arrived while the previous flush was running and flushes them together, so that one flush makes many events durable.

One run on this chapter’s machine: one event per flush made 5,950 events a second durable; batches of 10, 100 and 1,000 made 44,668, 332,406 and 1,958,725. A flush took 0.2 to 0.5 ms on this machine’s virtual disk, and can take ten times longer elsewhere, but the shape is the same. Replication adds a network round trip per batch, not per event, so the same batching carries over to consensus: the leader sends a batch, a majority stores it, and every transfer in it is committed at once. TigerBeetle, a database built for transfers, documents batches of up to 8,189 events per request for exactly this reason, “to amortize the overhead of consensus and I/O”.

Partitions multiply that. Fifteen partitions at 200,000 events a second each carry the estimate’s million transfers, with room for the extra events of crossing transfers. Two refinements matter in practice. A merchant that receives a large share of all transfers is a hot partition (); its incoming payments can be split over several sub-wallets that are merged when it withdraws. And transfers between wallets that usually trade with each other, a household, a company and its staff, can be kept in one partition to make them single events.

At ten times the transfers, the partitions multiply and the share of transfers that cross grows toward all of them, so each transfer costs nearly three events; keeping frequent pairs together is worth more. The log grows by about 50 TB a day, so old events move to object storage and snapshots keep restarts fast. At a hundred times, the relay and the read models, which see every event, need partitioning of their own ().

In the real world

  • Martin Fowler’s article on the LMAX architecture (12 July 2011) describes a business logic processor that “can handle 6 million orders per second on a single thread”, runs in memory using event sourcing, has its input journaled and replicated before processing, and takes a snapshot every night, rebuilding state by replaying events.
  • TigerBeetle’s documentation (as of October 2026) batches up to 8,189 accounts or transfers per request in its default configuration, to amortize consensus and I/O, and makes create events idempotent: the first with an id succeeds, later ones are told it exists. It also supports pending transfers that are posted or voided later, with timeouts.

In an interview. Start from the estimate: a million transfers a second does not fit one database, so wallets are partitioned and most transfers cross partitions. Compare two-phase commit, try-confirm-cancel and a saga for that, and pick the saga with an explicit in-flight state. Key points

  • A million transfers a second means partitions, and partitions mean most transfers cross from one to another.
  • Debit-then-credit loses money on a crash. Make the debit and the record of the transfer one event, and finish the transfer as an idempotent saga with money explicitly in flight.
  • Two-phase commit locks hot wallets through failures; a saga or a reservation of the amount does not.
  • Each partition is a replicated log of events applied in memory by one thread; balances are a fold over the log.
  • Keep apply deterministic, so replicas and replays agree; snapshots bound replay time.
  • Group commit, and batching in consensus, turn a flush per event into a flush per thousand.
  • Reads come from read models fed by the events, each saying how fresh it is.

Check yourself

Why does splitting wallets over more partitions increase the number of events per transfer?

With wallets spread evenly over P partitions, a transfer’s two wallets are in different partitions with probability 1 − 1/P. A transfer inside a partition is one event; across partitions it is three: send, receive, settle. So more partitions push the average toward three.

The process crashes after the sender’s partition recorded SendStarted but before the receiver was credited. What happens?

Nothing is lost. On restart the relay finds the transfer unsettled in the sender’s log and delivers it. If the receiver had already been credited, it recognizes the transfer id and ignores the repeat. Then the sender’s partition records SendCompleted.

Why is two-phase commit a poor fit for a wallet that receives a hundred transfers a second?

Each transfer locks both wallets until the coordinator decides, and if the coordinator crashes after preparing, the locks stay until it recovers. Every transfer to that wallet queues behind them; in the test, average waits reached 22 seconds.

What does “money in flight” mean, and why must it be explicit?

Money taken from the sender whose credit to the receiver is not yet settled. Counting it explicitly keeps the invariant checkable at every moment: all balances plus money in flight add up to the same total. Hidden, the books would appear not to balance while transfers complete.

A developer adds a fee to apply() that depends on the time of day. What breaks?

Replicas apply events at slightly different times and replays happen later, so the same events give different balances. In the test, two replicas diverged. The fee must be decided in decide() and stored in the event.

Why does a snapshot not change the answer of a replay?

A snapshot is the state as of a known event number, produced by the same apply function. Replaying from it applies the remaining events to the same starting point a full replay would have reached. The test compared state hashes and found them identical.

How does group commit raise throughput without weakening durability?

A transfer is still confirmed only after its event is flushed; but one flush covers every event that arrived while the previous flush was running. The cost of a flush is shared by the batch, so in the run here a thousand events per flush gave over 300 times the throughput of one.
