When one transaction is not enough

ended with a boundary: a transaction covers one database and nothing outside it. Systems cross that boundary constantly. Placing an order in the photo app’s print shop might involve four changes owned by four services:

  1. The order service records the order.
  2. The inventory service reserves the print.
  3. The payment service charges the card.
  4. The shipping service schedules delivery.

The business requires that these happen together. A customer must not be charged for an order that was never recorded, and stock must not stay reserved for an order whose payment failed. No single COMMIT spans the four.

The problem appears in its smallest form whenever a service changes its database and also needs to tell someone else.

This is the dual-write problem. It is not rare. A process can crash, be redeployed or lose its network connection between any two lines of code, and at thousands of requests per second the gap between two writes is hit regularly. Wrapping the code in try and catch does not help, because the failure may be that the process no longer exists to run the handler.

There are three families of solution: make the systems commit together, accept that they commit separately and repair failures, or arrange for there to be only one write.

Two-phase commit

Two-phase commit (2PC) is the classical way to make several databases commit as one. A coordinator manages the transaction, and the databases taking part are participants. It proceeds in two rounds.

  1. Prepare. The coordinator asks every participant whether it can commit. Each does all the work short of committing: checks constraints, takes locks, writes the changes to its log. Then it votes yes or no. A yes is a binding promise that it will be able to commit if told to, even after a crash.
  2. Commit or abort. If every vote is yes, the coordinator records the decision to commit in its own log and tells all participants to commit. If any vote is no, or a participant does not answer, it tells all of them to abort.

The protocol does achieve atomicity. Its weakness is what happens in the shaded region. A participant that has voted yes is in doubt: it has promised to commit if asked, so it cannot abort on its own, and it has not been told to commit, so it cannot do that either. If the coordinator crashes at that moment, the participant must wait for it to come back, holding its locks the whole time. Every other transaction that needs those rows waits too. 2PC is therefore called a blocking protocol.

The costs in practice:

  • Availability multiplies down. The transaction needs the coordinator and every participant to be up. By the arithmetic of , four components at 99.9% each give 99.6% together.
  • Latency adds up. Two rounds of messages and several forced disk writes, with locks held throughout.
  • The coordinator is a single point of failure unless its log is itself replicated.
  • Every participant must support the protocol. Databases do, through a standard called XA. Most message queues, third-party APIs and payment providers do not.

For these reasons 2PC across independent services has fallen out of favor. It remains the right tool in one place: inside a distributed database. Spanner, CockroachDB and similar systems use it to commit a transaction that touches several partitions, and they remove the blocking problem by making every participant, and the coordinator’s decision, a consensus group (). A coordinator that fails is replaced by another replica that has the same log. The database does this for you, and you see an ordinary transaction.

Sagas

The alternative to committing together is to commit separately and undo on failure. A saga is a sequence of local transactions, each in one service, each committing on its own. For every step there is a compensating transaction that reverses its effect. If a step fails, the saga runs the compensations for the steps already completed, in reverse order.

A saga gives atomicity in the end: either all steps complete or all completed steps are compensated. It does not give isolation. Between the first step and the last, the system is in an intermediate state that everyone can see. The order exists and is unpaid. The stock is reserved for an order that may be canceled. Designing a saga is mostly designing for that visibility.

  • Use explicit states. An order is pending until the saga finishes, and other code treats pending orders accordingly: not shown as confirmed, not shipped, not counted as revenue.
  • Compensation is not rollback. A rollback makes it as if nothing happened. A compensation is a new action: a refund, a cancellation notice. If an email was sent, the compensation is another email. History shows both.
  • Order the steps by how reversible they are. Put steps that are easy to undo first and the one that cannot be undone last. Charging a card can be refunded. Handing a parcel to a courier cannot be reversed. Once the irreversible step has succeeded, the saga must go forward, retrying the remaining steps until they succeed.
  • Compensations must succeed eventually. They are retried until they do, so they must be safe to repeat (), and a compensation that keeps failing needs to alert a person.

Something has to decide what happens next. There are two styles.

Choreography looks appealingly decoupled and becomes hard to follow as steps are added, since the business process exists only as the sum of many handlers. For anything with more than a few steps, orchestration is usually easier to reason about and to debug. covers the event-driven style in more depth.

type Step = { name: string; run: () => Promise; undo: () => Promise };

async function runSaga(steps: Step[]): Promise {
  const completed: Step[] = [];
  try {
    for (const step of steps) {
      await step.run();
      completed.push(step);
    }
  } catch (err) {
    for (const step of completed.reverse()) {
      await retryUntilSuccess(step.undo);       // a compensation must eventually succeed
    }
    throw err;
  }
}

await runSaga([
  { name: "create order",  run: () => orders.create(order),      undo: () => orders.cancel(order.id) },
  { name: "reserve stock", run: () => inventory.reserve(order),  undo: () => inventory.release(order.id) },
  { name: "charge card",   run: () => payments.charge(order),    undo: () => payments.refund(order.id) },
  { name: "confirm order", run: () => orders.confirm(order.id),  undo: async () => {} },
]);

This shows the shape and leaves out the hard part. The list of completed steps is in memory, so if the orchestrator crashes halfway, nothing remembers that a compensation is owed. A real orchestrator records its progress durably after every step, so that a restarted instance can continue or compensate. That is exactly what a workflow engine provides, which a later section describes.

The transactional outbox

Sagas need services to tell each other what happened, which brings back the dual-write problem: a step must update its database and send a message, atomically. The transactional outbox solves this by turning two writes into one.

Instead of sending the message, the service inserts it into an outbox table in its own database, in the same transaction as the business change. Both rows commit together or neither does. A separate process, the relay, reads the outbox and publishes each message to the queue.

BEGIN;

INSERT INTO orders (id, user_id, total_cents, status)
VALUES (5512, 42, 4200, 'pending');

INSERT INTO outbox (id, topic, payload)
VALUES ('01929f5e-7b1a-7c3e-9d2f-4a6b8c0d1e2f', 'order.created',
        '{"orderId": 5512, "userId": 42, "totalCents": 4200}');

COMMIT;

async function relayOnce(): Promise {
  const rows = await db.query(
    "SELECT id, topic, payload FROM outbox WHERE published_at IS NULL ORDER BY created_at LIMIT 100");

  for (const row of rows) {
    await queue.publish(row.topic, row.payload, { messageId: row.id });
    await db.query("UPDATE outbox SET published_at = now() WHERE id = $1", [row.id]);
  }
}

The relay has the same gap in miniature: it can crash after publishing and before marking the row. On restart it publishes that message again. So the outbox guarantees that every message is delivered at least once, never that it is delivered exactly once. That is an acceptable trade, because a duplicate is something the receiver can detect, using the message ID, and a lost message is not. Consumers must be written to tolerate duplicates.

The relay can find new rows by polling, as above, or by reading the database’s change log, which avoids polling delay and load. The second approach is .

In production. The outbox is the most widely useful pattern in this chapter. Any time a service must change its data and notify something else, such as a search index, a cache, another service or an email sender, write the notification to an outbox in the same transaction. It removes a whole class of “the database says one thing and everything else says another” incidents.

Reserve, then confirm

A saga’s lack of isolation is most awkward for scarce things: stock, seats, money. A useful refinement is to split each participant’s work into a tentative step and a final one, a pattern sometimes called try-confirm-cancel.

  • Try. Reserve the resource without consuming it: place a hold on one item of stock, or on an amount of money. The reservation has an expiry time.
  • Confirm. When every participant has reserved successfully, turn each reservation into the real thing.
  • Cancel. If anything fails, release the reservations. Anything left behind by a crash expires on its own.

You have met this as a customer. A hotel or fuel pump places a hold on a card and charges the final amount later. An airline holds a seat for ten minutes while you pay. The expiry is what makes the pattern robust: a reservation that nobody confirms or cancels cleans itself up, so a crashed coordinator cannot leave stock locked forever. The cost is that every participant must support reservations as a concept.

Workflow engines

An orchestrated saga needs durable progress tracking, retries with backoff, timeouts and timers, and visibility into what is stuck. Building these well is a project in itself. Workflow engines, also described as durable execution, provide them as a platform. You write the process as ordinary code or as a state machine, and the engine records every step’s result so that the process survives crashes and resumes exactly where it stopped, even days later. Temporal and AWS Step Functions are widely used examples.

For multi-step business processes, especially ones that wait on people or external systems, a workflow engine is usually a better foundation than hand-written saga code. covers them.

The backstop: reconciliation

However carefully a saga is built, some sequence of failures will eventually leave two systems disagreeing. A bug in a compensation, a message dropped by a misconfiguration, a provider that reports success and later reverses it. Mature systems assume this and check.

Reconciliation is a periodic job that compares two sources of truth and reports or repairs the differences: every charge at the payment provider against every paid order, every reserved item against an open order, every outbox row against what consumers received. Financial systems have done this for centuries under the name of balancing the books. It is unglamorous and it is what turns “eventually consistent, we hope” into “eventually consistent, and we know”. builds a design around it.

Common mistake. Handling only the failure you can see. Code that calls a payment API, and on an error response cancels the order, has handled a declined card. It has not handled a timeout, where the charge may or may not have happened, or a crash after the charge and before the next line. Each step of a multi-system operation has three outcomes, success, failure and unknown, and the third needs an answer: retry with an idempotency key, or look up the result.

In an interview. When a flow crosses services, say how it stays consistent: “this is a saga, orchestrated by the order service, with the order in a pending state until payment confirms, and the stock reservation released if payment fails”. Mention the outbox for publishing events, that consumers are idempotent, and a reconciliation job. Proposing two-phase commit across microservices usually leads to a question about what happens when the coordinator dies.

Common mistakes

  • Writing to a database and a queue in sequence and hoping nothing fails in between.
  • Treating a multi-service operation as atomic because the code is in one function.
  • Sagas with no compensations, or with compensations that are never tested.
  • Compensations that are not safe to repeat.
  • No pending state, so half-finished work looks finished to the rest of the system.
  • The irreversible step placed early in the sequence.
  • Saga progress kept only in memory.
  • Ignoring the “unknown” outcome of a remote call.
  • No reconciliation, so drift is found by customers.
  • Service boundaries that cut through data needing a transaction.

Key points

  • A transaction covers one database. Writing to two systems in sequence can always leave them disagreeing.
  • Two-phase commit makes several participants commit together and blocks, holding locks, if the coordinator fails at the wrong moment. It belongs inside distributed databases.
  • A saga is a series of local transactions with compensations. It is atomic in the end and not isolated, so intermediate states must be designed for.
  • Order saga steps from most to least reversible, and make compensations safe to repeat.
  • The transactional outbox removes the dual write: save the message in the same transaction as the change, and publish it afterward, at least once.
  • Reservations with an expiry let scarce resources take part safely.
  • Record saga progress durably, or use a workflow engine that does.
  • Prefer keeping data that changes together in one database, and run reconciliation to catch what slips through.

Check yourself

A service saves an order and then publishes an event. Why does swapping the order of those two operations not fix the dual-write problem?

A crash between the two steps leaves them inconsistent either way. Database first, and the order exists with no event. Event first, and other services act on an order that was never saved. The fix is to make it one write, by putting the event in an outbox table in the same transaction.

In two-phase commit, a participant has voted yes and the coordinator crashes. Why can the participant not simply abort after a timeout?

The coordinator may have received all yes votes, logged a commit decision and told other participants to commit before crashing. If this participant aborted, some would have committed and it would not, breaking atomicity. It has to wait to learn the decision, holding its locks.

A saga has steps: reserve stock, send confirmation email, charge card. What is wrong with this order?

The email cannot be unsent, and it comes before a step that can fail. If the charge is declined, the customer has been told the order is confirmed. Irreversible steps should come after the steps that might fail, so the charge goes before the email.

What does a saga not provide that a database transaction does, and how do designs cope?

Isolation. Other requests can see the partial state between steps. Designs cope with explicit statuses such as pending, which the rest of the system treats as not yet real, and with reservations that hold a resource without committing it.

The outbox relay publishes a message and crashes before marking it as published. What happens next, and what does the consumer need?

On restart the relay finds the row still unpublished and publishes it again, so the consumer receives the message twice. The consumer must detect the duplicate, typically by remembering the IDs of messages it has processed, and skip it.

Why might a design review suggest merging two services instead of adding a saga between them?

If the two services’ data must frequently change together atomically, the service boundary is cutting through a transaction. A saga adds compensations, intermediate states and failure cases to recover what a single local transaction gives for free. Keeping that data in one service and one database removes the problem.
