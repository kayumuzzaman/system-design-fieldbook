The problem

The problem. Design a system that counts clicks on online ads. Each click sends the user on to the advertiser’s page and is recorded. Advertisers see clicks per ad per minute on a dashboard within a minute or so, and are billed for their clicks each day. The billed numbers must be exact: no click lost and none counted twice, although phones send clicks late and more than once, and machines crash.

A counter on one machine is a line of code. The difficulty is in three facts about real events. They arrive out of order and sometimes hours late, so “the clicks of 12:00” is never known to be complete. They arrive more than once, because clients and pipelines retry. And the machines doing the counting crash, so a count must survive being computed twice. A fast answer is needed for dashboards and an exact one for billing, and the design produces both from the same events and compares them. In the terms of , this is counting a stream.

An invalid click is a click that does not come from a person’s real interest in the ad: an accident, a bot, or someone clicking to run up a competitor’s costs. Advertisers do not pay for them, so the count that is billed is the count after they are removed.

The rate is modest for a partitioned log (), and the raw volume is ordinary for object storage. The surprise is the third line. Counting by minute shrinks a billion clicks only to about 279 million rows, because most ads are clicked less often than once a minute, and a row for one click saves nothing. So the store must handle hundreds of millions of rows a day, and queries over long ranges should read the hourly and daily rows. Summing fine-grained counts into coarser ones, minutes into hours and hours into days, is a rollup. The popularity curve is an assumption; the calculator lets you change it.

Every count is derived. The raw clicks are the truth, kept long enough that any count can be computed again from them, which is what makes the rest of the design safe to get slightly wrong for a while.

Step 5: The design

The click servers do as little as possible: check the signature, append one record to the log and redirect. The stream aggregator reads the log, removes repeated ids, counts by ad and minute of event time, and writes counts to the results store as each minute closes. A second consumer copies raw clicks to the archive. Every hour a batch recount reads the archive and counts again, exactly and without time pressure, and reconciliation compares the two. This is the lambda pattern () kept for one reason: money.

The simulation behind the deep dives generates 600,000 clicks over two hours on 20,000 ads, with the most popular ad drawing 9.6% of them. Its delays are assumptions meant to resemble a mobile ad network: 95% of clicks arrive within about a second, 4.5% within a minute, as apps send in batches, and 0.5% between a minute and two hours late, from phones that were offline. Two percent of clicks are sent a second time, most within seconds and one in ten when the app is next opened, up to six hours later. That added 12,209 copies.

Step 6a: When is a minute finished?

Clicks are counted in the minute they happened, which needs the event time and the windows and watermarks of . That chapter explains the mechanism; here is what it does to one ad’s count.

The watermark delay is a dial between speed and completeness, and the simulation measured it. With the first result written the instant a minute ended, 3.72% of clicks arrived after their window had closed. Waiting 10 seconds left 2.25% late, and a minute 0.69%. After that the curve is almost flat: waiting ten minutes still left 0.60%.

The flat part is the offline phones. No reasonable wait catches a click that arrives two hours late, and every second of waiting delays every count on every dashboard. So the design separates the two: a short watermark, 10 seconds, for the first result, and a long allowed lateness, the time a stream processor keeps a closed window’s state so that a late event can still update it, of 2 hours. With that setting, no click in the simulation was dropped: 235,254 first results were followed by 13,484 corrections, one for each late click. The results store therefore receives counts as totals that replace earlier ones, never as increments, and the dashboard labels recent minutes provisional. Flink, for one, drops late events by default: its allowed lateness is zero unless set.

Step 6b: The same click twice

Copies come from phones that retry, from click servers that resend after a timeout, and from the log itself, which delivers at least once (). Because every copy carries the click id made when the ad was shown, the aggregator can remove them by remembering the ids it has seen. The question is for how long, since every remembered id costs memory.

Ten minutes removes nine copies in ten for a fraction of the memory. The rest arrive hours later and are left to the batch recount, which can afford to compare every id of the day. A Bloom filter would hold far more ids in the same memory, but its false positives would discard real clicks as repeats (): acceptable for a trending list, not for an invoice.

Removing repeats only works if all copies of a click reach the same aggregator. The log is partitioned by ad and the click id carries its ad, so they do.

Step 6c: Counting exactly through crashes

An aggregator does three things with each batch it reads: it counts, it writes the counts to the store, and it records the offset it has read up to (). It can crash between any two. The simulation ran the same 600,000 clicks through a consumer that crashed 20 times at random points, with the three steps in different orders.

  • Record the offset first. A crash after recording loses the batch: 10,000 clicks never counted.
  • Write increments, then record the offset. A crash after writing replays the batch, and its increments are added again: 4,500 clicks counted twice.
  • Write totals with a version, and save the offset and the counts together. After a crash the aggregator restores both from its last checkpoint () and reads again from that offset. It produces exactly the same totals as before, and the store ignores a total whose version it already has. All 600,000 were counted once.

No step happened only once in the third case: batches were read, counted and written more than once. What holds is that each click affects the result once, which is what “exactly once” means in practice (). Stream processors offer it as a checkpoint plus an output that is either transactional, written only when the checkpoint completes, or idempotent, as the versioned totals are here.

Step 6d: Checking the stream against a recount

The stream’s counts are fast and nearly right. The batch recount is slow and right: it runs after the hour is over, so nothing is late, and it can remove every repeat of the day. Reconciliation compares them count by count ().

For the two simulated hours, with a 10-second watermark, 2 hours of allowed lateness and ids remembered for 10 minutes, the stream matched the recount in 237,778 of 238,921 ad-and-minute counts. None was too low. The other 1,143 were too high by 1,234 clicks in total, and the test checks that this is exactly the number of copies that arrived 10 minutes or more after the first copy of their click. A reconciliation that cannot explain its differences is a fire alarm; one that explains every one of them is how the team knows the stream is correct. With ids remembered for 7 hours, every count matched.

What the business does with the difference is a policy. Here the batch is the referee: billed totals come from the recount, dashboards show the stream until the hour is final, and the store marks reconciled minutes as final. The batch also applies the slower checks for invalid clicks that need a whole day of a device’s or a network’s activity.

One ad goes viralIts partition falls behindCombining at the click servers, below

Partitioning by ad sends all of one ad’s clicks to one partition. That keeps its copies together, and it also makes the popular ad a hot key ().

In the simulation of one billion clicks a day over 2 million ads, the busiest of 32 partitions took 9.0% of all messages, about three times its share. The fix is pre-aggregation: combining events into partial counts before they are sent on, so that the next stage receives a count instead of each event. If each of 50 click servers sends one message per ad per second, carrying a count, a viral ad becomes at most 50 messages a second, and the busiest partition fell to 3.4%. The total fell by only 19%, because most ads are clicked less than once a second on any one server. The raw clicks still go to the archive one by one, and the ids travel with the partial counts so that repeats can still be removed.

At ten times the volume, 10 billion clicks a day, the peak reaches 580,000 a second: more partitions and aggregators, and the same design. The id memory grows to about 14 GB, spread across the aggregators. At a hundred times, the usual reason is impressions rather than clicks. Exact counts of impressions are still needed for billing by impression, but questions such as how many different people saw an ad switch to sketches (), and the store keeps fine-grained rows for days and only rollups after that.

In the real world

  • Uber described its ad event pipeline on 23 September 2021: Flink jobs reading and writing Kafka with exactly-once settings, results committed at each checkpoint, clicks and impressions counted in one-minute windows keyed by ad and minute, and a record id used as an idempotency key, with Pinot’s upsert so that a record is never stored twice. It named the checkpoint interval, two minutes, as the main limit on freshness.
  • Google’s Photon, described at SIGMOD 2013, joins web search queries with ad clicks across data centers. Its output never contains duplicates and becomes exactly once eventually; it handled millions of events a minute at peak with an average end-to-end latency under 10 seconds, and survives the loss of a data center without manual intervention.
  • Google Ads says invalid clicks are filtered out of advertisers’ reports and billing, and that clicks found invalid after billing are credited back (help page, checked October 2026).
  • In Apache Flink, a window’s allowed lateness is zero by default, and elements later than that are dropped (API documentation, checked October 2026).

Key points

  • Recording a click is one durable append and a redirect; all counting happens later, from the log.
  • Count by when a click happened. A short watermark gives fast first results; allowed lateness turns late clicks into corrections instead of losses.
  • The delay curve has two parts: seconds of network, which a watermark handles, and hours of offline phones, which only corrections or the batch catch.
  • Remove repeats by click id, for as long as memory allows; the batch removes the rest.
  • Through crashes, write totals with versions and checkpoint the offset with the counts. Increments after a replay double count.
  • Recount exactly from raw clicks, reconcile, and be able to explain every difference.
  • Partitioning by ad makes a popular ad a hot partition; combining at the source flattens it.

Check yourself

A click happened at 12:00:58 and arrived at 12:01:03. In which minute is it counted, and does it count if the watermark trails by 10 seconds?

In 12:00, the minute it happened. It counts: when it arrives at 12:01:03, no click seen so far can have happened later than that, so the watermark is at most 12:00:53, before the end of the minute, and window 12:00 is still open.

Why not set the watermark delay to two hours, so that almost nothing is late?

Because every first result would then appear two hours after its minute, and dashboards would be useless. The simulation showed that beyond a minute, waiting longer gains almost nothing, because the remaining late clicks come from offline phones hours later. Allowed lateness gets the same completeness with results after 10 seconds.

What must the results store support for allowed lateness to work?

Replacing a count it already holds: the aggregator sends the new total for a minute, keyed by ad and minute, and the store overwrites the earlier one. Consumers of the store must accept that recent values change, which is why the API marks them provisional.

An aggregator writes increments and then commits its offset. It crashes between the two. What happens, and how do you fix it?

It restarts from the old offset, counts the same batch again and adds the increments a second time: clicks counted twice, 4,500 in the test’s 20 crashes. Write totals with a version instead, and save the offset and the counts in one checkpoint, so a replay rewrites the same totals and the store ignores versions it has.

Ids are remembered for 10 minutes. Which repeated clicks get through, and who catches them?

Copies that arrive 10 minutes or more after the first copy of their click, such as a queue resent when the app is next opened. In the simulation that was 1,234 clicks. The hourly batch recount removes every repeat across the whole day, and reconciliation shows the difference.

Why is a Bloom filter a poor choice for removing repeated clicks before billing?

Its errors go in the wrong direction. A false positive says “seen” for an id that was never seen, so a real click is thrown away and the advertiser is under-billed with no record of why. Exact ids for a bounded time, plus the batch, never discard a real click.

One ad gets a tenth of all clicks. What does that do to a log partitioned by ad, and what helps?

Its partition receives far more than its share, and the aggregator reading it falls behind while others idle: 9.0% against an average of 3.1% in the simulation. Combining at the click servers, one message per ad per server per second with a count, caps the hot ad at one message per server per second and brought the busiest partition to 3.4%.
