Agreement, despite everything

Earlier chapters kept arriving at a decision that several machines had to make identically. Which replica is the leader? Has this transaction committed? In what order did these writes happen? Who holds the lock? If two machines answer differently, the result is split brain, lost data or a double booking.

Consensus is the problem of getting a group of machines to agree on a value so that the agreement holds even though some machines fail and the network misbehaves. A correct consensus algorithm guarantees:

  • Agreement. No two machines decide differently.
  • Validity. The value decided is one that some machine proposed.
  • Durability of the decision. Once decided, it does not change.
  • Termination. Eventually a decision is reached, as long as enough machines are working and able to communicate.

Agreeing on a single value sounds limited. In practice the machines agree on a sequence of values, one after another, which gives them an identical ordered log. If every machine starts in the same state and applies the same operations in the same order, they all end in the same state. This technique is called state machine replication, and it turns consensus into a general tool: put any operations in the log, such as database writes, configuration changes or lock grants, and every replica stays identical.

Why it is hard

established that a slow machine cannot be told from a dead one. That is the root of the difficulty. If a leader stops responding, the others must choose a new one. If it was only slow, there are now two machines that each believe they lead.

A famous result from 1985, known by its authors’ initials as FLP, proves that no deterministic algorithm can guarantee consensus in a system where messages can be delayed without limit and even one machine may crash. That sounds like the end of the matter. The way through is to separate two kinds of guarantee:

  • Safety: nothing bad ever happens. Two machines never decide differently.
  • Liveness: something good eventually happens. A decision is reached.

Practical algorithms keep safety always, whatever the network does. They give up liveness only while the network is misbehaving: during a bad partition they may fail to make progress, and they never produce a wrong answer. When the network settles, they continue. For a system holding locks or money, that is the right way round. An answer that is late is recoverable, and two contradictory answers are not.

The idea that makes it possible: majorities

The mechanism underneath every practical consensus algorithm is the majority quorum. A decision counts only when more than half of the machines have accepted it. Two facts follow.

  • Any two majorities share at least one machine. So two conflicting decisions cannot both gather a majority, since the shared machine would have had to accept both. And any later majority includes at least one machine that knows about every earlier decision.
  • A minority cannot decide anything. During a partition, at most one side has a majority. The other side waits. This is what makes split brain impossible.

Raft, step by step

Raft, published in 2014, is the consensus algorithm most systems use today, and it was designed to be understandable. It works by electing a leader and having that leader manage a replicated log.

Roles and terms

Each server is in one of three roles. A follower accepts instructions. A leader handles all client requests and tells followers what to add to their logs. A candidate is a follower trying to become leader.

Time is divided into numbered terms. Each term begins with an election and has at most one leader. The term number is Raft’s logical clock, in the sense of : every message carries it, a server that sees a higher term than its own adopts it immediately, and a server that receives a message from a lower term rejects it. This one rule is how an out-of-date leader finds out that it has been replaced.

Electing a leader

  1. The leader sends a heartbeat to every follower several times a second.
  2. Each follower runs a timer, set to a random duration, typically between 150 and 300 ms. Every heartbeat resets it.
  3. If a follower’s timer runs out, it assumes the leader is gone. It increases its term, becomes a candidate, votes for itself and asks the others for their votes.
  4. Each server grants at most one vote per term, to the first candidate that asks, provided that candidate’s log is at least as complete as its own.
  5. A candidate that collects votes from a majority becomes leader and starts sending heartbeats.

The random timers keep elections from colliding. Usually one follower times out first and wins before any other becomes a candidate. If two do become candidates together and split the votes, neither reaches a majority, both time out after different random delays, and one of them wins the next term.

The condition on votes is what protects data. A candidate whose log is missing committed entries cannot win, because a majority holds those entries and each of those servers will refuse it. The new leader is therefore guaranteed to have everything that was ever committed.

Replicating the log

With a leader in place, a write proceeds as follows.

  1. The client sends the write to the leader.
  2. The leader appends it to its own log and sends it to all followers.
  3. Each follower appends it and acknowledges.
  4. When a majority, counting the leader, has stored the entry, it is committed. The leader applies it to its state and replies to the client.
  5. The leader tells followers the entry is committed in its next message, and they apply it too.

The leader does not wait for every follower, only for a majority, so one slow or failed server does not hold up writes. A follower that falls behind, or returns after a crash, is brought up to date by the leader, which finds the last position where their logs agree and sends everything after it. Raft maintains a strong property that makes this simple: if two logs contain an entry with the same position and term, the logs are identical up to that point.

What happens in a partition

This is the scenario that broke the simpler failover schemes in , and Raft’s rules handle it without special cases. The old leader keeps running and cannot commit, because it cannot reach a majority. Its clients receive timeouts, not false confirmations. The majority side elects a new leader with a higher term. When the partition heals, the first message the old leader receives carries that higher term, and it becomes a follower. Its uncommitted entries are overwritten with the new leader’s log. Nothing that was acknowledged to any client is lost.

Paxos and the rest of the family

Raft is not the only algorithm, or the first. Paxos, devised by Leslie Lamport around 1990, was the first proven solution, and it has a reputation for being hard to understand and harder to implement correctly. Google’s Chubby and Spanner use it. ZooKeeper uses a related protocol called Zab. Viewstamped Replication, from 1988, is closer to Raft than to Paxos in structure.

For a system designer these are equivalent in what they provide: a replicated log with a single leader at a time, committed by majorities, safe under any failure of a minority. They differ in details that matter to implementers. Raft’s contribution was a complete, clearly specified design, and it is the reason there are now good open-source implementations in every major language.

What consensus costs

  • Latency on every write. A write is not acknowledged until a majority has stored it durably. With three replicas in three availability zones, that adds a millisecond or two. With replicas on three continents it adds the round-trip time to the second-nearest replica, often over a hundred milliseconds.
  • A single leader. All writes go through one machine per group. Systems that need more write capacity run many independent groups, one for each partition of the data, which is how CockroachDB, TiDB and YugabyteDB are built ().
  • Unavailability without a majority. If two of three machines are down or unreachable, the group stops accepting writes. This is the CP choice of .
  • Reads are not free either. A leader that has been partitioned away may not know it yet, so a truly up-to-date read requires the leader to confirm with a majority that it still leads, or to rely on a time-bounded lease.
  • Leader changes cause a pause. After a leader fails, nothing is committed until a follower times out and wins an election. That is typically a second or so, depending on the configured timeouts.
  • Sensitivity to slow disks. Each server must write to disk before acknowledging. A consensus cluster on slow or contended storage produces spurious elections.

Where you meet consensus

You will almost never write a consensus algorithm, and should not. It is among the easiest things in computing to get subtly wrong. You use it through systems that embed it.

Fencing: when a leader does not know it was replaced

Consensus guarantees that the group agrees on who the leader or lock holder is. It cannot guarantee that the previous holder knows. showed a node that checked its lease, was paused, and acted on the stale check. The algorithm was correct throughout, and two nodes still wrote.

The protection has to be at the resource being written to. Each time the lock or leadership is granted, the coordination service issues a fencing token, a number that only ever increases. The holder sends its token with every write. The storage system remembers the highest token it has seen and rejects anything lower.

async function writeWithToken(key: string, body: string, token: number): Promise {
  const result = await db.query(
    `UPDATE documents
        SET body = $1, fencing_token = $2
      WHERE key = $3 AND fencing_token <= $2`,       // accept only the current or a newer holder
    [body, token, key]);

  if (result.rowCount === 0) throw new StaleLockHolder();
}

Raft’s term number is a fencing token for its own messages, which is why an old leader’s instructions are ignored. The same idea must be extended to anything outside the consensus group that the leader touches. A distributed lock without fencing provides efficiency, by usually preventing duplicate work, and does not provide correctness. Where fencing at the resource is impossible, the remaining option is to make certain the old holder has stopped, by cutting its power or its network access.

A note on Byzantine consensus

Everything above assumes machines that fail by stopping and never by lying. Agreement among machines that may send false or contradictory messages is Byzantine fault tolerant consensus. It needs more than two thirds of the machines to be honest, where the algorithms above need only a majority to be alive, and many more messages. Blockchains are the best-known use, since their participants do not trust each other. Inside one organization’s infrastructure, where every machine runs software you deployed, it is almost never worth the cost.

Using consensus well

  • Use three or five members, placed in separate failure domains: three availability zones is the standard arrangement.
  • Keep the group within one region unless you need to survive the loss of a region, and accept the write latency if you do.
  • Put little in it. Use the consensus store for decisions and metadata, and keep bulk data elsewhere.
  • Give it fast, dedicated disks and watch the time its disk writes take.
  • Monitor leader changes. Frequent elections mean timeouts are too tight for the hardware or the network.
  • Expect clients to see errors during an election, and retry.
  • Fence anything a leader does outside the group.

Common mistake. Building leader election from parts that look sufficient: “each instance writes its ID to a database row with a timestamp, and whoever wrote most recently is the leader”. Such schemes work until a pause, a clock skew or a partition produces two leaders. Use a coordination service that implements consensus, and add fencing tokens.

In an interview. You are not expected to derive Raft. You are expected to know when consensus is needed and what it implies: “leader election goes through etcd, which needs a majority of three nodes, so we lose availability and not correctness if two zones fail”. If you propose a lock, mention fencing. If asked how failover avoids split brain, the answer is “a majority must agree on the new leader, and a higher term or token makes the old one’s writes get rejected”.

Common mistakes

  • Two-node or four-node groups, expecting more fault tolerance than they give.
  • Home-made leader election based on timestamps or a shared database row.
  • Locks without fencing tokens, trusted for correctness.
  • Using the consensus store as a general database or a message queue.
  • Spreading one group across distant regions without accounting for write latency.
  • All members in one availability zone, so one failure removes the majority.
  • Assuming a leader’s read is current without a majority check or a lease.
  • Election timeouts tuned so tightly that a slow disk causes leadership to change repeatedly.

Key points

  • Consensus lets a group of machines agree on a sequence of operations despite crashes and lost messages. Applying the same log everywhere keeps replicas identical.
  • Practical algorithms are always safe and make progress whenever a majority can communicate.
  • Majorities are the core idea: any two overlap, and a minority can decide nothing. Use three or five members.
  • Raft elects one leader per numbered term, replicates a log, and commits an entry when a majority has stored it.
  • A partitioned leader cannot commit, and steps down when it sees a higher term. Acknowledged writes are never lost.
  • The cost is a majority round trip on every write, a single leader per group, and no writes without a majority.
  • Use consensus through etcd, ZooKeeper or a database that embeds it. Do not write your own.
  • A leader or lock holder can be replaced without knowing. Fencing tokens checked at the resource make that harmless.

Check yourself

Why does a four-member consensus group tolerate no more failures than a three-member one?

A majority of four is three, so only one member can be lost, the same as with three members where the majority is two. The fourth member adds cost and another thing that can fail without adding tolerance.

In Raft, how does a leader that was cut off by a partition learn that it is no longer the leader?

Every message carries a term number. The other side elected a new leader in a higher term. When the old leader next exchanges a message with any server that has seen the higher term, it adopts that term and becomes a follower. Until then it could not commit anything, having no majority.

A client writes to a Raft leader, which appends the entry and crashes before any follower receives it. Is the write lost, and was the client misled?

The entry is lost, since it existed only on the failed leader. The client was not misled: the leader replies only after a majority has stored the entry, so the client received no confirmation and should retry.

Why can a candidate with an out-of-date log not win an election?

Servers refuse to vote for a candidate whose log is less complete than their own. Every committed entry is on a majority of servers, and any majority of voters includes at least one of them, so a candidate missing a committed entry cannot gather enough votes.

What do “safety” and “liveness” mean, and which does a consensus algorithm sacrifice when the network is badly partitioned?

Safety means nothing incorrect happens, such as two different decisions. Liveness means progress is eventually made. Under a severe partition the algorithm may stop making progress, giving up liveness for a while, and it never gives up safety.

A service uses a lock from a coordination service before writing to object storage that has no way to check tokens. What risk remains?

A holder that is paused past its lease expiry can resume and write after another instance has taken the lock, and the storage will accept both writes. Without fencing at the resource the lock only reduces the chance of duplicate work. To be safe, the resource must reject stale holders, for example with a conditional write on a version, or the old holder must be forcibly stopped.
