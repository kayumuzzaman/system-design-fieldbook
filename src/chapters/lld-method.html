What is being asked

separated two levels of design. High-level design decides which services, stores and queues exist. Low-level design decides what is inside one of them: the classes, what each knows, what each may do and how they call each other. In an interview it takes the form “design a parking lot” or “design a vending machine”, and you are expected to produce a class diagram and working code for the central part in about 45 minutes.

The problems are deliberately familiar. Nobody is testing your knowledge of parking. They are watching how you get from a vague sentence to a structure, and the things they look for are few and consistent.

The method

The method has seven steps. The order matters more than the exact minutes: each step produces what the next one needs.

StepYou produceShare of the time
1. ClarifyThree to five use cases, and a list of what is out of scope12%
2. Find the modelEntities, values and the main operations, as a list12%
3. State the invariantsTwo to four rules that must always hold, each with an owner6%
4. Draw the classesA class diagram of five to eight classes with relationships16%
5. Decide what variesThe one or two interfaces the requirements call for, by pattern name14%
6. Code the coreThe main use case, in code that would run, with its checks28%
7. Walk it and extend itOne scenario traced through, then concurrency, testing and extensions12%

Of 45 minutes, 40 are yours. Spend about 5 on questions, have the class diagram drawn by minute 21, and be writing code by minute 27. That leaves about 11 minutes of coding, enough for one use case and its checks. Stop at minute 38 whatever state the code is in, and trace a scenario.

Two checkpoints matter most. If the class diagram is not on the board by the time shown, stop adding classes and connect the ones you have. If you are still coding at the last checkpoint, stop and walk the scenario with what exists. An unfinished method with a clear explanation is worth more than finished code that nobody had time to discuss.

The rest of this chapter applies the seven steps to one problem.

The problem. Design the lending system for a library. Members borrow and return books. The library owns several copies of each title. A member may hold at most three books at once, and a loan lasts 14 days. A late return costs a fine for each day. A member who owes too much cannot borrow.

Step 1: Clarify

A problem statement is short on purpose. The first minutes are for finding its edges, and the useful questions fall into four groups.

Ask aboutFor the libraryAn assumption to state if not told
Who uses it, and for whatIs this the librarian’s desk system, or do members use it directly?One caller, a desk application. No login in scope
The core actionsBorrow, return, pay a fine. Also search? Reservations? Renewals?The three named. Reservations as an extension if time allows
The numbers and what variesAre the limit, the loan period and the fine fixed, or different for each branch or kind of member?They are configuration. One policy now, designed so that it can vary
What is outStorage, user interface, payment processing, notificationsEverything in memory. Paying a fine is a number going down

Write the answers down where the interviewer can see them, as use cases: “a member borrows a title”, “a copy is returned and a fine may be charged”, “a member pays toward their fines”. That list is the definition of done for the next forty minutes. Asking nothing and asking for ten minutes are both mistakes. The first builds the wrong thing, and the second leaves no time to build anything.

Steps 2 and 3: The model and its invariants

Go through the statement and the use cases, and sort the words as in . Three decisions made at this point shape everything after.

  • Split the overloaded noun. “Book” means two things here. What a member asks for is a title. What leaves the building is one physical copy. Rules attach to copies: this copy is on loan, that one is free. Most problems have such a word, and finding it early is the largest single improvement to a design. In a parking lot it is the spot and the ticket. In a cinema it is the seat and the booking.
  • Promote the relationship. A loan connects a member and a copy, and it has data of its own: when it started and when it is due. A relationship with data of its own is a class.
  • Collect the numbers. Three books, fourteen days, a fine per day and a limit on fines owed are policy. They go together in one object, so that the rules read from it and a second library can supply different values.

Then say the invariants aloud, and give each one an owner: the class that holds the data needed to check it.

InvariantOwnerWhy that class
A copy is in at most one loan at a timeCopyIt holds its current loan, so it alone can refuse a second
A member holds at most three loansMemberIt holds the member’s loans
A member owing the limit or more cannot borrowMemberIt holds the amount owed
A loan is due a fixed number of days after it startsLoanIt computes the due date once, when created

This table is the most valuable thing produced in the whole exercise, and it takes two minutes. A design in which every rule has exactly one owner is nearly always a good one. A rule with no owner will be enforced by whichever caller remembers, and a rule with two owners will be enforced twice, differently.

Step 4: Draw the classes

The notation is that of , and an interviewer will accept any reasonable simplification of it. Three habits keep the drawing useful.

  • Draw the things before the coordinator. It is tempting to begin with a class named LibrarySystem and hang methods on it. That class then absorbs every rule, and the entities become records. Start from the entities and add the entry point last.
  • Put numbers on the lines. “0..3” on the line from member to loan is an invariant in two characters.
  • Stop at five to eight classes. If the board has fifteen, most are speculation. If it has two, one of them is doing everything.

Step 5: Decide what varies

Patterns enter a design at one point only: where the requirements, or the interviewer’s follow-up questions, say that behavior differs from case to case or will change. Listen for the phrases, and reach for the smallest thing that answers them.

You hearIt usually meansCovered in
“Different pricing, fees, rules or algorithms for different cases”Strategy: an interface or a function passed in
“It can be idle, busy, out of service…”, with different responses in eachA state table, or the state pattern if states have behavior
“Notify”, “when that happens, also…”Observer: events and subscribers
“Several kinds of vehicle, piece, shape or message” that are created from inputPolymorphism, with a factory where the kind is chosen
“Groups that contain groups”Composite
“Add logging, limits or retries around it”Decorator, or a chain of handlers
“Undo”, “queue the requests”, “replay”Command
“Use the payment provider, the map service, the printer”A port of your own, with an adapter behind it
Nothing of the kindNo pattern. A class with methods

In the library, one thing varies: the numbers. They do not need a strategy interface with classes. They need a record, LendingPolicy, passed to the library. If the interviewer then says “students pay no fines and staff may hold ten books”, the answer is a policy for each kind of member. If they say “fines double after a week”, the fine becomes a function in the policy, which is a strategy. Introduce each step when it is asked for, and say what you would change. Building all of it in advance costs minutes you do not have, and shows a weaker judgment than waiting.

Step 6: Code the core

There is never time to code everything, so choose. Write the use case that touches the most invariants, completely, including the refusals. For the library that is borrowing and returning. Leave out whatever is mechanical, such as listing the catalog, and say that you are leaving it out.

// Dates are whole days, counted from 1 January 1970. No clock is read anywhere in this file: "today" is passed in.
export type Day = number;
export const day = (iso: string): Day => Math.floor(Date.parse(`${iso}T00:00:00Z`) / 86_400_000);

export type MemberId = string & { readonly __brand: "MemberId" };
export type CopyId = string & { readonly __brand: "CopyId" };
export type Isbn = string & { readonly __brand: "Isbn" };

// What varies between libraries is data, kept in one place.
export interface LendingPolicy {
  readonly maxLoans: number;
  readonly loanDays: number;
  readonly finePerDayCents: number;
  readonly finesBlockAtCents: number;
}
export const standardPolicy: LendingPolicy = { maxLoans: 3, loanDays: 14, finePerDayCents: 25, finesBlockAtCents: 500 };

export class LendingError extends Error {}

export class Loan {
  readonly copy: CopyId;
  readonly member: MemberId;
  readonly borrowedOn: Day;
  readonly dueOn: Day;

  constructor(copy: CopyId, member: MemberId, borrowedOn: Day, loanDays: number) {
    this.copy = copy;
    this.member = member;
    this.borrowedOn = borrowedOn;
    this.dueOn = borrowedOn + loanDays;
  }
  daysLate(returnedOn: Day): number {
    return Math.max(0, returnedOn - this.dueOn);
  }
}

// Owns the rule "one copy is in at most one loan".
export class Copy {
  readonly id: CopyId;
  readonly isbn: Isbn;
  private loan: Loan | null = null;

  constructor(id: CopyId, isbn: Isbn) {
    this.id = id;
    this.isbn = isbn;
  }
  get available(): boolean {
    return this.loan === null;
  }
  get currentLoan(): Loan | null {
    return this.loan;
  }
  lend(loan: Loan): void {
    if (this.loan) throw new LendingError(`copy ${this.id} is already on loan`);
    this.loan = loan;
  }
  takeBack(): Loan {
    if (!this.loan) throw new LendingError(`copy ${this.id} is not on loan`);
    const loan = this.loan;
    this.loan = null;
    return loan;
  }
}

// Owns the rules about one member: how many loans, and how much is owed.
export class Member {
  readonly id: MemberId;
  readonly name: string;
  private readonly loans = new Set();
  private owedCents = 0;

  constructor(id: MemberId, name: string) {
    this.id = id;
    this.name = name;
  }
  get openLoans(): number {
    return this.loans.size;
  }
  get finesCents(): number {
    return this.owedCents;
  }

  // Returns the reason this member may not borrow, or null if they may.
  refusal(policy: LendingPolicy): string | null {
    if (this.loans.size >= policy.maxLoans) return `${this.name} already has ${policy.maxLoans} books`;
    if (this.owedCents >= policy.finesBlockAtCents) return `${this.name} owes ${this.owedCents} cents in fines`;
    return null;
  }
  take(loan: Loan): void {
    this.loans.add(loan);
  }
  handBack(loan: Loan, fineCents: number): void {
    if (!this.loans.delete(loan)) throw new LendingError(`${this.name} does not hold that loan`);
    this.owedCents += fineCents;
  }
  pay(cents: number): void {
    if (!Number.isInteger(cents) || cents <= 0 || cents > this.owedCents) throw new LendingError("pay a whole amount, up to what is owed");
    this.owedCents -= cents;
  }
}

// The entry point. It finds the objects, asks them, and keeps the two sides of a loan in step.
export class Library {
  private readonly policy: LendingPolicy;
  private readonly members = new Map();
  private readonly copies = new Map();

  constructor(policy: LendingPolicy = standardPolicy) {
    this.policy = policy;
  }

  join(id: string, name: string): Member {
    const member = new Member(id as MemberId, name);
    this.members.set(member.id, member);
    return member;
  }
  addCopy(id: string, isbn: string): Copy {
    const copy = new Copy(id as CopyId, isbn as Isbn);
    this.copies.set(copy.id, copy);
    return copy;
  }

  borrow(memberId: string, isbn: string, today: Day): Loan {
    const member = this.member(memberId);
    const refusal = member.refusal(this.policy);
    if (refusal) throw new LendingError(refusal);
    const copy = [...this.copies.values()].find((c) => c.isbn === isbn && c.available);
    if (!copy) throw new LendingError(`no copy of ${isbn} is available`);
    const loan = new Loan(copy.id, member.id, today, this.policy.loanDays);
    copy.lend(loan);
    member.take(loan);
    return loan;
  }

  // Returns the fine charged, in cents.
  giveBack(copyId: string, today: Day): number {
    const copy = this.copies.get(copyId as CopyId);
    if (!copy) throw new LendingError(`no copy ${copyId}`);
    const loan = copy.takeBack();
    const fine = loan.daysLate(today) * this.policy.finePerDayCents;
    this.member(loan.member).handBack(loan, fine);
    return fine;
  }

  member(id: string): Member {
    const member = this.members.get(id as MemberId);
    if (!member) throw new LendingError(`no member ${id}`);
    return member;
  }
  get allCopies(): readonly Copy[] {
    return [...this.copies.values()];
  }
  get allMembers(): readonly Member[] {
    return [...this.members.values()];
  }
}

Several earlier chapters are visible in this listing, and it is worth being able to point at each.

  • Private state, changed only by methods that check first: Copy.lend refuses a second loan, and Member.pay refuses a negative amount.
  • Types that prevent mix-ups. A CopyId cannot be passed where a MemberId is expected.
  • No hidden inputs. today is a parameter of every operation that depends on the date, so the tests below needed no fake clock ().
  • Errors in the language of the domain, of one type, with messages a librarian could read.
  • A thin coordinator. Library.borrow is nine lines: find, ask, create, tell. It decides nothing about loans or fines itself.

The code was tested in two ways. The first is the scenario of the next section, with results at each step. The second is a check of the invariants themselves: 20,000 random borrow, return and payment requests were thrown at a small library, and after every one the test confirmed that no member held more than three loans, that no amount owed was negative, and that the copies’ view of who holds what agreed with the members’. About half of the requests were refused, as they should have been, and the invariants held throughout.

Common mistake. Coding the easy parts first. Constructors, getters and an addBook method fill a screen and prove nothing. Start with the method that has an if in it for every invariant, because that is the one the design exists for.

Step 7: Walk it, then extend it

Tracing a scenario through the finished design is the cheapest test there is, and it regularly finds a method that was drawn and never written or an object that needs data it cannot reach. Use real values, as in the figure, and include one awkward case. In the tested scenario, Ana’s fourth loan was refused with “Ana already has 3 books”, a third request for a title with two copies was refused, a return on the due date cost nothing, one five days late cost 125 cents, and at 500 cents owed she was refused until she had paid.

Nine problems, a few shapes

Low-level design problems look more varied than they are. The nine that follow fall into a handful of shapes, and recognizing the shape tells you which part of the toolbox will carry the design.

ShapeProblemsWhat carries the design
Handing out a limited resource, One owner for “no two holders of the same thing”, and an atomic claim under concurrency
A machine with modes, A state machine, written as a table or as state objects
A data structure with a contract, The right structure for the required speed, behind a small interface, made safe for concurrent use
Rules about money between peopleValue objects, strategies for the rules, and totals that must add up
A gamePolymorphic pieces, a board that validates moves, and a turn-taking state machine
A small library that other code builds onInterfaces first: observer, strategy and a chain of handlers

Each chapter follows the seven steps in order, with the problem as an interviewer would state it, the questions to ask, the model and its invariants, the diagram, the code with its tests, and the follow-up questions.

In an interview. Say the plan in your first minute: “I’ll ask a few questions, list the main objects and the rules they must keep, sketch the classes, then code the central use case and walk through it.” It tells the interviewer you have a method, and it gives them the chance to redirect you before you spend time on a part they do not care about. Then keep talking as you work. A correct design produced in silence is hard to give credit for.

Key points

  • A low-level design problem is assessed on scoping, modeling, where the rules live, readiness for change, working code and communication.
  • Work in seven steps: clarify, find the model, state the invariants, draw the classes, decide what varies, code the core, walk it and extend it.
  • Have the class diagram done in about the first half of the time, and stop coding with enough left to trace a scenario.
  • Look for the overloaded noun, such as book for both title and copy, and split it.
  • State two to four invariants and give each exactly one owning class. That table is the design.
  • Draw entities before the coordinating class, and keep the coordinator thin.
  • Add an interface or a pattern only where the requirements say behavior varies.
  • Code the use case with the most rules in it, including every refusal.
  • Prepare the shape of the four standard follow-ups: concurrency, testing, a new feature and storage.

Check yourself

You are asked to design a hotel’s room reservation system. Which noun in that sentence is overloaded, and how would you split it?

“Room”. A guest asks for a kind of room, such as a double with a sea view, for a range of dates. What is finally assigned is one physical room. Rules attach to each differently: availability is counted for each room type and night, while cleaning and maintenance belong to a physical room. Model a room type and a room, with the reservation connecting a guest, a type, dates and, later, a specific room.

In the library design, why does the check “this copy is not already on loan” live in Copy and not in Library.borrow?

Copy holds the data the rule depends on, its current loan, privately. Placed there, the rule is enforced however a loan is attempted, including by a method added to Library next year. Placed in borrow, it protects only that one path, and the copy itself would accept a second loan from any other caller.

Twenty minutes into a 45-minute round you have twelve classes on the board and no relationships between them. What do you do?

Stop adding. Pick the five or six that the main use case needs, draw the lines between them with multiplicities, and say aloud that the rest are deferred. Then state the invariants and move to code. A diagram finished by about the halfway point matters more than a complete inventory of everything that could exist.

The interviewer says: “Fines should be waived for members over 65, and doubled for books that someone else has reserved.” What changes in the library design?

The fine stops being a number in the policy and becomes a function of the loan, the member and the day returned: a strategy. giveBack calls it where it now multiplies. Copy, Member and Loan do not change, apart from exposing whatever the new rule needs to read, such as the member’s age band. Say that out loud. It shows the design absorbing the change.

Which of these call for a pattern, and which do not: “the system must support three kinds of member”, “members get an email when a reserved book comes back”, “the library has one catalog”?

Three kinds of member with different limits call for a policy for each kind, which is data or a small strategy, and not three subclasses of Member. The email on return is an observer: returning announces an event and the notifier subscribes. One catalog calls for nothing. Create it once and pass it in. It is not a reason for a singleton.

Why code the refusals, such as “already has 3 books”, when time is short?

Because they are the invariants, and the invariants are what the design is for. Success-path code shows only that objects can call each other. The checks show that the rule exists, where it lives and that it holds on every path. They also give you the edge cases to name when asked how you would test.

Two desks try to lend the last copy of a title at the same instant, in a system backed by a database. Outline your answer.

The shared state is the copy’s availability, and the invariant is one open loan for each copy. Reading “is it free?” and then inserting a loan are two steps with a gap between them. Close the gap in the database: a unique constraint over the copy for loans that have not been returned, so the second insert fails, or a conditional update that marks the copy as lent only if it is free. The loser gets a conflict and is told the copy has already gone.
