The problem

The problem. Design a stock exchange. Brokers send orders to buy and sell shares of listed companies, and cancel them. The exchange matches buyers with sellers, tells both sides about every trade at once, and publishes every change to its order books to anyone who subscribes. It must be fair to the microsecond, never lose an order it has acknowledged, and keep running through the failure of any one machine.

Most designs in this part spread work over many machines that each do a little, and accept some disagreement between them. An exchange cannot. Two orders that arrive a microsecond apart must be handled in that order, and every copy of the system must agree on what traded, to the share and the cent. So the heart of the design is deliberately small: one numbered stream of orders, and one thread for each book applying it. Everything around that heart exists to feed it quickly, to copy it exactly and to tell the world what it did. In the terms of , this is ordering at low latency.

A limit order is an order to buy or sell a quantity at a stated price or better, which waits in the book if it cannot trade at once. A market order trades immediately at the best prices available, and whatever cannot trade is canceled rather than left waiting.

The numbers make three decisions (). Everything fits in the memory of one ordinary server, so the books live in memory. Even one thread for all symbols has 2.3 µs per message at the peak, so there is no need to share a book between threads, and good reason not to: locks would cost more than the work. And a disk write takes longer than that budget, so no message can wait for its own write to disk; the journal is written in large sequential batches, or copied to a backup’s memory, as describes.

Step 3: The API

Real exchanges take orders over long-lived TCP sessions in binary protocols, such as Nasdaq’s OUCH, or in FIX, a text protocol shared across the industry. The shapes below are written as HTTP for readability; the content is the same.

Three details matter. The client order id is unique within the broker’s session, so a broker that reconnects and sends an order again gets the original back instead of a second order (). Prices travel as decimal text or whole ticks, never as floating-point numbers, because 10.01 has no exact binary form. And every stream is numbered: execution reports, so a broker can ask for what it missed after a reconnect, as FIX sessions do with a resend request; and market data, so a subscriber can tell that a snapshot taken at 11 continues with message 12. A cancel can lose a race: if the order traded a microsecond earlier, the answer is a conflict, not an error in the exchange.

The order book is the list of resting buy and sell orders for one symbol, arranged by price. It is never written to a database. It is derived state: the journal is the truth, and the book is what applying the journal produces, which is applied to the busiest object in the system. The highest buy price is the best bid, the lowest sell price the best ask, and the gap between them is the spread.

Step 5: The design

Brokers connect to order gateways, which hold their sessions and reject any order that breaks the broker’s limits: a price far from the market, a size too large, too much exposure in total. Checks belong here, before sequencing, so that the engine never spends its budget on orders it will refuse. Gateways forward what passes to the sequencer, which gives each message the next number, makes it durable and sends the numbered stream to the engine and its backups. The matching engine is the component that holds the order books and turns the sequenced stream of orders into trades, applying one message at a time. Its output goes three ways: execution reports back through each party’s gateway, market data to every subscriber, and trades to the systems that settle them later.

The path is short on purpose: three hops, all inside one building, each done by a program that holds everything it needs in memory. No step waits on a database, and no step after the sequencer makes a decision that depends on anything but the stream.

Step 6a: The order book and matching

The rule that decides who trades is price-time priority: an incoming order trades first with the best price on the other side, and among resting orders at the same price, with the one that arrived first. The trade happens at the resting order’s price, so a buyer willing to pay 10.02 who finds a seller at 10.01 pays 10.01.

The data structure follows from the rule. Each side of the book keeps its prices sorted, best first, and each price holds a level: a queue of orders, oldest first, kept as a doubly linked list with a running total. A map from order id to its place in a queue makes a cancel a matter of unlinking one node. Matching only ever touches the front of the best queue.

out.push({ type: "accepted", id });
    const opposite = cmd.side === "buy" ? this.asks : this.bids;
    let left = cmd.qty;
    while (left > 0) {
      const level = opposite.best();
      if (!level) break;
      if (cmd.type === "limit" && (cmd.side === "buy" ? level.price > cmd.price : level.price < cmd.price)) break;
      const maker = level.head as Resting;         // the oldest order at the best price trades first
      const qty = Math.min(left, maker.qty);
      left -= qty; maker.qty -= qty; level.total -= qty;
      out.push({ type: "trade", taker: id, maker: maker.id, side: cmd.side, price: level.price, qty, makerLeft: maker.qty });
      if (maker.qty === 0) {
        level.unlink(maker);
        this.byId.delete(maker.id);
        if (level.count === 0) opposite.drop(level);
      }
    }

Adding at an existing price, canceling and each fill take constant time. Only a new price costs more: a binary search and an insert among the levels, of which there are dozens to hundreds near the market. When prices stay in a known band, an array indexed by tick removes even that.

Fast code that is subtly wrong is worse than slow code here, so the test checks the book against a naive matcher that sorts every candidate again for each incoming order. Over 400,000 random commands in 200 seeded runs, mixing resting orders, crossing orders, market orders and late cancels, both produced identical events: 140,088 trades, 209,791 orders resting, 47,691 cancels and 72,377 refusals, almost all of them cancels that arrived after their order had traded. After every command the book was not crossed, meaning the best bid stayed below the best ask, and at the end every share of every order was accounted for as traded, canceled or still resting.

Step 6b: One order for everything

Price-time priority needs a time, and in a distributed system there is no shared clock to read it from (). Two gateways stamping orders with their own clocks would disagree by more than the gaps between orders. The answer is a sequencer: the single component through which every order and cancel passes, which assigns each one the next number and so defines the order in which everything happened. “Time” in the book is that number. The engine is then a state machine whose input is the numbered stream, the technique of applied to one busy machine.

apply(input: Sequenced, out: Output[] = []): Output[] {
    // A gap or a repeat means this replica has not seen exactly the input the others saw: stop, do not guess.
    if (input.seq !== this.lastSeq + 1) throw new Error(`expected input ${this.lastSeq + 1}, got ${input.seq}`);
    this.lastSeq = input.seq;
    let book = this.books.get(input.symbol);
    if (!book) { book = new OrderBook(); this.books.set(input.symbol, book); }
    const events: BookEvent[] = [];
    book.submit(input.cmd, input.seq, events);
    for (const event of events) {
      out.push({ seq: input.seq, time: input.time, symbol: input.symbol, event });
      this.outHash = digest(this.outHash, event);
    }
    return out;
  }

For this to work, the engine must be deterministic: no reading of the clock, no random numbers, no iteration over anything whose order could change. The time it needs arrives inside the message, stamped by the sequencer. Then any copy that applies the same stream reaches the same state. The test sequenced 200,000 random commands for eight symbols to a primary and a backup engine, replayed the journal into a third, and restored a fourth from a snapshot taken at command 100,000 before applying the rest. All four ended with the same fingerprint of their books and the same checksum of the 388,577 events they had produced. An engine given a gap or a repeated number refuses it.

What happens without one agreed order is easy to underestimate, so the chapter measured it. Two gateways each sent 300 orders for one stock, at about 100,000 a second each, to two replicas over separate paths that delayed each message by a random amount. Each replica matched in its own order of arrival.

With delays of up to a quarter of a microsecond, the replicas made different trades in 12% of runs. At one microsecond it was 36%, and at 20 microseconds every run. Only a small share of orders was affected, 89 of 84,026 at one microsecond, but one is enough: a backup that would have filled a different order cannot take over, and a replay cannot reproduce the day. With a sequencer, the order is decided once and every copy reads it.

The sequencer is now the one component everything waits for, so it must be fast and must not lose what it has numbered. The design used by LMAX, described below, is to send each message to the backups and to the journal in parallel and let the engine process it only once both have it. The order is acknowledged after that, so a crash at any moment loses nothing that a broker was told about. Copying to a backup’s memory over a local network costs microseconds; waiting for a disk to confirm each message would cost far more. So the journal is written to disk in large batches, and until a batch is on disk, the copy in the backup’s memory is what protects each message (). If the sequencer fails, a standby continues from the last number in the journal.

Orders for different symbols never interact, so the exchange partitions by symbol: each group of symbols has its own sequencer, engine and backup (). What cannot be split is one symbol: its book must stay on one thread, and the busiest symbol sets the limit. The calculator’s “share on the busiest engine” is that limit.

Step 6c: Market data

Market data is the stream of public information about an exchange’s books and trades. It comes in three levels of detail: the best bid and ask (level 1), the total quantity at each price (level 2), and every individual order as it is added, executed or deleted (level 3). The exchange publishes the most detailed one, and the other two are derived from it, which is what the test’s subscriber does. Every change becomes one message: in a run of 100,000 commands the engine produced 103,983, of which 41,862 were orders added to the book, 56,492 executions against resting orders and 5,629 deletions. Orders that trade on arrival never appear as added orders, only through the executions against the orders they hit.

Thousands of subscribers want the same messages at the same moment, so the feed is sent by multicast: the network delivers one transmission to every receiver that has joined the group, and the sender does no work per receiver. It runs over UDP (), so there are no acknowledgments and a packet can be lost. Sequence numbers make that safe.

The test encoded the messages in the binary layouts of Nasdaq’s ITCH feed, packed them into packets with a header in the style of MoldUDP64, and dropped packets at random. With 1% lost, the subscriber found 23 gaps and repaired them with 23 requests; with 5% lost, 129 gaps, fewer than the 132 lost packets because neighbors lost together form one gap. Each time, the depth it rebuilt equaled the engine’s exactly. A subscriber that joins late, or falls too far behind, does not ask for the whole day: it loads a snapshot stamped with a sequence number and applies the feed from the next number on.

The binary messages averaged 32.4 bytes against 80.0 for the same content as JSON, and with packet headers the feed came to 36.3 bytes for each command, the figure the estimate uses. Size matters less than what the receiver must do with each byte, which is the subject of the last deep dive.

In production. Exchanges sell colocation: space for a firm’s servers in the exchange’s own data center, connected by cables of the same length, so that the feed reaches every colocated firm at the same moment. Fairness at this scale is partly a question of cable lengths.

Step 6d: Designing for microseconds

At the estimated peak one engine has about 2.3 µs for each message. The chapter’s engine, which is ordinary TypeScript running in Node, matched one million random commands at 1.67 million a second in one run, 598 ns each on average. So matching is not the problem. Everything around it can be.

  • One thread, no locks. A book is touched by one thread only, so it needs no locks and never waits for another thread. Throughput comes from partitioning symbols, not from sharing a book.
  • Memory only, and no wait for the disk per message. The engine holds everything it needs. Durability comes from the sequencer’s copies, done before the engine sees the message.
  • Fixed binary layouts, in buffers that are reused. Encoding and parsing one message as JSON took 1,825 ns in the same run, three times the cost of matching. The binary layout took 961 ns with a new buffer for every message and 166 ns writing into one buffer that was reused: most of the cost was allocating memory, not the format.
  • No pauses. The median command took 606 ns, including about 75 ns spent reading the clock twice, and the 99th percentile 1.8 µs. But the 99.9th percentile was 17.4 µs, and the slowest single command 2.8 ms. A pause of the whole program, such as the runtime collecting memory, lands on whichever order happens to be in flight. Production engines avoid creating garbage on this path, give each engine thread a processor core of its own, and read the network without going through the operating system’s usual stack.
  • Short distances. Light covers 200 km in a millisecond in fiber (): 5 µs per kilometer. A single round trip across an ordinary data center network, about half a millisecond (), is two hundred times the budget. So the parts of the critical path sit on one short network, and colocated firms sit beside them.

The tail matters more than the average, for the reason gives: at 427,000 messages a second, a once-in-a-thousand pause happens hundreds of times a second, and during each one a queue builds that the next messages must wait behind.

At ten times the volume, 10 billion messages a day, the peak is 4.3 million a second. Spread over 16 engines, with the busiest taking a tenth of the traffic, each message on that engine has 2.3 µs again; the feed reaches 1.2 Gbit/s and is split into several multicast groups by symbol. At a hundred times, the busiest symbol alone may exceed what one thread can do, and nothing in this design splits one book. That is where exchanges move the hottest paths into hardware, and where the rules of the market, such as minimum price steps and limits on message rates, become part of the capacity plan.

In the real world

  • LMAX, a trading platform in London, ran its business logic on a single thread handling 6 million orders a second; input was journaled and copied to replicas before processing, and a full restart from a snapshot and a day’s journal took less than a minute (Martin Fowler, 12 July 2011).
  • Nasdaq’s TotalView-ITCH 5.0 specification (revision of 28 April 2023) defines the order-by-order feed used in the test: an add-order message of 36 bytes, an execution of 31 and a delete of 19, timestamps in nanoseconds since midnight and prices with four implied decimals, delivered over MoldUDP64. MoldUDP64 numbers the first message of each packet, sends heartbeats with a count of zero about once a second, and answers re-requests by unicast.
  • Nasdaq says its INET trading platform handles more than one million messages a second “at sub-40 microsecond speeds” (an undated page, checked in October 2026).
  • IEX delays incoming orders by 350 microseconds on purpose, using coiled optical fiber, 38 miles of it until it moved its primary data center in 2024 and 44 miles since, to keep the same delay (as of October 2026).
  • CME Globex supports nine matching algorithms (page updated 11 February 2025), among them first-in-first-out, which is price-time priority, and several pro-rata variants that share a fill among orders at a price in proportion to their size.
  • US markets pause all trading for 15 minutes if the S&P 500 falls 7% or 13% in a day before 3:25 p.m., and stop for the day at 20%; a single stock pauses for five minutes if its price stays outside a band around its recent average for 15 seconds (SEC, checked October 2026).
  • On 1 August 2012, Knight Capital, a broker, sent more than 4 million orders in 45 minutes while trying to fill 212 customer orders, and lost more than $460 million; the SEC found it had violated the rule requiring brokers to check orders before they reach an exchange (SEC, 16 October 2013).

Key points

  • The estimate decides the design: at the peak an engine has a couple of microseconds per message, so books live in memory and nothing waits on a disk.
  • Price-time priority maps onto price levels, each a queue of orders oldest first, with an id map for constant-time cancels.
  • Check a fast book against a naive one over many random flows; the chapter’s matched exactly over 400,000 commands.
  • One sequencer defines the order; a deterministic engine then makes every replica, replay and restored snapshot identical.
  • Without one order, a microsecond of jitter made two correct replicas trade differently in about a third of runs.
  • Market data goes out once, by multicast, numbered; receivers repair gaps by re-request and catch up from snapshots.
  • Matching is the cheap part. Text formats, allocation, pauses and distance are what break a budget of microseconds.

Check yourself

A buy order for 500 at 10.05 arrives. The asks are 300 at 10.03 (order 12), 100 at 10.03 (order 15), and 400 at 10.04. What trades?

300 at 10.03 with order 12, then 100 at 10.03 with order 15, which arrived later at the same price, then 100 at 10.04. The buyer pays each resting order’s price, never its own limit of 10.05, and the order is completely filled, so nothing rests.

Why is the order book not stored in a database that the engine updates?

The book changes with almost every message, and the engine has a couple of microseconds for each; a database write takes far longer. The book is derived state: the journal is durable, and the book can be rebuilt by applying it, from the start or from a snapshot.

Two gateways each stamp orders with their own accurate clocks. Why is that still not enough to decide priority?

Clocks on different machines disagree by more than the time between orders, which at hundreds of thousands a second is microseconds. Two orders could be stamped in the opposite order to their arrival, and two replicas reading them could disagree. A single sequencer’s numbers define one order that every copy shares.

The engine needs the time to expire day orders at the close. Why may it not read its own clock?

Because a replay or a backup would read a different clock and could expire orders at a different point in the stream, producing different trades. The time must come with the stream: the sequencer stamps every message, or inserts a “close” message, and the engine acts on that.

When is an order acknowledged to the broker, and why then?

After the sequencer has made it durable, on the journal and in the backup, and the engine has processed it. Acknowledging earlier would mean a crash could lose an order the broker believes was accepted. Making each message durable by copying it to a backup’s memory keeps that wait to microseconds.

A subscriber receives messages 1 to 3, then 7 to 9. Why must it not apply 7 to 9 at once?

Messages 7 to 9 may execute against or delete orders added in 4 to 6, which it has not seen, so its book would be wrong or it would fail on unknown orders. It keeps 7 to 9 aside, asks for 4 to 6, and applies everything in order.

Matching takes 0.6 µs on average. Why can the engine still fall behind at a rate it could sustain on average?

Because of the tail. A pause of tens of microseconds to milliseconds builds a queue that every following message waits behind, and at hundreds of thousands of messages a second even rare pauses happen many times a second. The design must remove pauses, not only make the average fast.
