A log instead of a queue

The queues of the previous chapter delete each message when a consumer acknowledges it. That suits jobs: once a thumbnail is made, the request for it has no further use. It suits records of what happened less well. “Photo 871 was uploaded” is of interest to the thumbnail service, the search indexer, the analytics pipeline, the fraud detector and a recommendation model that will not be written until next year. With a queue, each of them needs its own copy, arranged in advance, and anything that arrives late has missed the past.

A log takes a different approach. It is a sequence of records that is only ever appended to. Each record is given the next position number, called its offset. Reading does not remove anything. Each reader simply remembers the offset it has reached.

Three properties follow, and they are the reason logs spread through the industry after Apache Kafka was released by LinkedIn in 2011.

  • Many independent readers. Adding a consumer costs the producers and the other consumers nothing. Nobody has to be asked.
  • Replay. A reader can go back. A consumer with a bug can be fixed and rerun over the last day’s records. A new service can start from the oldest record and build its state from history.
  • Order. Records in one log are read in exactly the order they were written, by every reader, every time.

The model also has costs. Records occupy disk until they expire, whether or not anyone reads them. A consumer cannot acknowledge one record and skip another: its progress is a single position. And parallelism is fixed by how the log is divided, as the next section shows.

The idea is not new. It is the write-ahead log of and the replication log of , taken out of the database and offered as a service.

Topics and partitions

A single log has a single writer position and lives on one machine’s disk, which limits how much it can take. So a named stream of records, called a topic, is split into several logs called partitions. Each partition is an independent log with its own offsets.

The producer decides which partition each record goes to. If the record has a key, the partition is chosen by hashing the key, exactly as in . If it has no key, records are spread evenly.

This gives the same bargain as ordering by key in a queue, made structural.

  • Within a partition, order is exact. Everything with the same key is in one partition, so all events for one user, one order or one device are read in sequence.
  • Across partitions, there is no order. If your design needs events for different keys in a global sequence, a partitioned log cannot provide it, short of using a single partition.

The key should be the thing whose events must stay in order: usually the ID of the entity the event is about. A badly chosen key has the same consequences as a badly chosen shard key. If one key carries a large share of the traffic, its partition becomes hot while others sit idle ().

Brokers and replication

The servers that store partitions are called brokers. A cluster spreads partitions across its brokers, and keeps several copies of each for safety, typically three.

For each partition, one replica is the leader and the rest are followers. Producers write to the leader. Followers continuously fetch new records from it. This is the leader-follower replication of , applied to one partition at a time, with different brokers leading different partitions so that the load is spread.

The leader tracks which followers are keeping up. That set, including the leader itself, is the in-sync replicas, or ISR. A follower that falls too far behind is dropped from the set until it catches up. A record counts as committed once every in-sync replica has it, and the offset up to which records are committed is the high watermark. Consumers are only ever given committed records, so they cannot read something that a failover might take back.

The producer chooses how much assurance to wait for, with a setting called acks.

SettingThe producer is told “done” whenRisk
acks=0It has sent the record, without waiting for any replyRecords vanish without notice
acks=1The leader has written itLost if the leader fails before followers copy it
acks=allEvery in-sync replica has itNone, provided enough replicas were in sync

The proviso matters. If two followers have fallen out of sync, the in-sync set is the leader alone, and acks=all then means “the leader has it”. A second setting closes the gap: min.insync.replicas makes the leader refuse writes when the in-sync set is smaller than a minimum. The standard production configuration is three replicas, a minimum of two in sync and acks=all. It tolerates the loss of any one broker with no data loss and no interruption, and on the loss of two it stops accepting writes instead of risking them. Since Kafka 3.0, acks=all has been the producer’s default.

Something must keep track of which brokers are alive and choose a new leader when one fails. In Kafka that is a small group of controller nodes that agree using Raft. Until version 4.0, released in March 2025, it was an external ZooKeeper cluster ().

Producers: batching and safe retries

A producer does more than send. Three behaviors shape throughput and correctness.

Batching. Sending each record in its own network request would be slow. The producer collects records bound for the same partition and sends them together, compressed as one unit. It waits a few milliseconds for a batch to fill. In Kafka the wait is linger.ms, which has defaulted to 5 ms since version 4.0, and the target size is batch.size, 16 KB by default. A longer wait gives larger batches, better compression and higher throughput, at the cost of that much added latency. This is the latency-for-throughput trade of as a pair of configuration values.

Retries without duplicates. If a send times out, the producer cannot know whether the broker wrote the batch. Retrying risks writing it twice, and a retry overtaking a later batch risks reordering. Kafka’s idempotent producer solves both. Each producer has an ID and numbers its batches for each partition, and the broker discards any batch it has already written. It has been on by default since 3.0.

Routing. The producer asks any broker for the cluster’s metadata, learns which broker leads each partition, and sends directly to the right one. There is no proxy in the data path.

Why a log on disk is fast

Storing every message on disk sounds slow. A log is fast because of how it uses the disk.

  • Appends only. Writes go to the end of a file. Sequential writes are the fastest thing a disk does, on spinning disks and SSDs alike, and they need no index maintenance ().
  • Segments. A partition is a series of files called segments, 1 GB each by default in Kafka. Only the newest is written to. Deleting old data means deleting whole files, which costs almost nothing.
  • A sparse index. Each segment has a small index from offset to file position, so a reader can jump to any offset with one lookup and then read forward.
  • The operating system’s cache. Recently written data is still in memory in the page cache. Consumers that keep up are served from memory without the broker managing a cache of its own.
  • One format end to end. A batch is stored as the producer sent it, still compressed, and handed to consumers the same way. The broker does not decode and re-encode records, and can pass file contents to the network without copying them through the application.

The result is that a broker’s throughput is close to that of its disks and network, commonly hundreds of megabytes a second.

Retention and compaction

A log cannot grow forever. There are two policies for what to discard.

Retention by time or size. Segments older than a limit, seven days by default, or beyond a total size are deleted. This suits streams of events, where old events matter less. A consumer that falls further behind than the retention period loses data, so retention must exceed the longest outage you intend to survive.

Compaction. For a topic that records the changing state of things, such as each user’s current profile, most old records are obsolete: only the latest value of each key matters. A compacted topic keeps at least the most recent record for every key and removes older ones in the background.

A compacted topic is, in effect, a table that can be replayed. It is how Kafka stores consumer offsets internally, and it is the basis for the change streams of .

Disk on brokers is expensive compared with object storage, so recent Kafka versions can move older segments to an object store and fetch them back on demand. This tiered storage makes long retention affordable. Several newer systems go further and write the log directly to object storage, giving up some latency for much lower cost and simpler operation.

Consumers and consumer groups

A consumer reads by asking a broker for records from an offset. It pulls. The broker does not push. Pulling lets each consumer take records at the rate it can handle, in batches, which builds backpressure into the protocol: a slow consumer simply asks less often and falls behind, without being overwhelmed.

To share the work of reading a topic, consumers join a consumer group. The group’s partitions are divided among its members so that each partition is read by exactly one member at a time. Different groups are entirely independent: each has its own offsets and reads everything.

Two consequences deserve attention.

Partitions cap parallelism. A group cannot usefully have more members than the topic has partitions. With four partitions, a fifth consumer waits idle as a spare. The partition count must therefore be chosen for the consumer capacity you will need at peak.

Membership changes cause rebalances. When a consumer joins, leaves or is judged dead, partitions are reassigned. In older versions of the protocol the whole group stopped reading while this happened, and a group whose members kept timing out could spend most of its time rebalancing. Newer protocols move only the partitions that need to move, and since Kafka 4.0 the assignment can be computed by the broker. Rebalances remain something to watch. A common cause is a consumer that takes so long over one batch that it misses its deadline for asking for the next, five minutes by default, and is presumed dead ().

Offsets and what they guarantee

A group’s progress is a committed offset for each partition, stored by the brokers. When a consumer starts, or takes over a partition, it resumes from the committed offset. When the consumer commits decides the delivery guarantee.

Order of operationsIf the consumer crashes in betweenGuarantee
Commit, then processThe records were marked done and never processedAt most once
Process, then commitThe records are read again and processed twiceAt least once

Process-then-commit is the right default, with consumers written to tolerate repeats (). Be careful with clients that commit automatically on a timer. If the commit fires while records are still being processed in the background, a crash loses them.

The whole model fits in a few dozen lines, which is worth reading once to see how little a consumer’s state amounts to.

interface LogRecord { offset: number; key: string; value: string; }

class Partition {
  private records: LogRecord[] = [];

  append(key: string, value: string): number {
    const offset = this.records.length;            // offsets are positions: 0, 1, 2, ...
    this.records.push({ offset, key, value });
    return offset;
  }

  read(fromOffset: number, max: number): LogRecord[] {
    return this.records.slice(fromOffset, fromOffset + max);   // reading removes nothing
  }

  get endOffset(): number { return this.records.length; }
}

function hashKey(key: string): number {
  let h = 2166136261;
  for (let i = 0; i < key.length; i++) h = Math.imul(h ^ key.charCodeAt(i), 16777619);
  return h >>> 0;
}

class Topic {
  private readonly partitions: Partition[];
  private readonly committed = new Map();     // "group/partition" -> next offset to read

  constructor(partitionCount: number) {
    this.partitions = Array.from({ length: partitionCount }, () => new Partition());
  }

  // The same key always lands in the same partition, so its records stay in order.
  send(key: string, value: string): { partition: number; offset: number } {
    const partition = hashKey(key) % this.partitions.length;
    return { partition, offset: this.partitions[partition].append(key, value) };
  }

  poll(group: string, partition: number, max = 100): LogRecord[] {
    return this.partitions[partition].read(this.position(group, partition), max);
  }

  // The consumer records how far it has got. This is the only state a reader has.
  commit(group: string, partition: number, nextOffset: number): void {
    this.committed.set(`${group}/${partition}`, nextOffset);
  }

  lag(group: string, partition: number): number {
    return this.partitions[partition].endOffset - this.position(group, partition);
  }

  private position(group: string, partition: number): number {
    return this.committed.get(`${group}/${partition}`) ?? 0;
  }
}

const uploads = new Topic(3);
const { partition } = uploads.send("user-42", "photo 871 uploaded");
uploads.send("user-42", "photo 872 uploaded");                 // same key: same partition, next offset

for (const record of uploads.poll("thumbnails", partition)) {
  // ... do the work for this record, then:
  uploads.commit("thumbnails", partition, record.offset + 1);
}
uploads.lag("thumbnails", partition);                          // 0: caught up
uploads.lag("search-indexer", partition);                      // 2: a new group starts from the beginning

The last two lines show the measurement that matters most when running consumers: consumer lag, the distance between the end of the log and a group’s committed offset. Lag that holds steady is healthy. Lag that grows means the group cannot keep up, and it is the streaming equivalent of the age of the oldest message in a queue.

Exactly once, within limits

Kafka can go beyond at-least-once for one specific shape of work: read from topics, compute, and write to other topics. Its transactions let a consumer’s offset commit and the records it produced be written atomically, so that a crash leaves either both or neither. Stream processors build on this ().

The guarantee stops at the edge of the log. If processing a record sends an email or charges a card, no transaction in the broker can undo that on a retry. Effects on outside systems still need idempotency keys, as explained.

Queue behavior on a log

The consumer group model has two limits that queues do not have. Parallelism cannot exceed the partition count. And because progress is one offset per partition, a record that cannot be processed blocks everything behind it in that partition. The usual workaround for the second is to publish the failing record to a separate retry topic, commit past it and carry on, with a dead-letter topic for records that fail repeatedly.

Kafka 4.2, released in February 2026, made a second consumption model ready for production use: share groups, also known as queues for Kafka. Members of a share group can read from the same partition together. Each record is acknowledged individually, redelivered if its consumer does not acknowledge it in time, and has its delivery attempts counted. Ordering is given up in exchange. One topic can now serve both an ordered consumer group and a pool of workers treating it as a queue.

Sizing a topic

Two numbers have to be settled before a topic is created: how much disk it needs, and how many partitions.

The topic takes in 5 MB a second, or 432 GB a day. Keeping 7 days on 3 replicas needs about 9.07 TB of disk before compression. One consumer handles 500 messages a second, so a group needs at least 10 consumers to keep up, and therefore at least 10 partitions. Plan for the peak you expect: adding partitions later changes which partition each key maps to.

Partition count is a decision that is awkward to revisit.

  • Too few caps consumer parallelism, and one partition’s throughput caps what a single key can receive.
  • Too many costs open files, memory, replication traffic and recovery time when a broker fails.
  • Adding later is possible but changes the result of hash-modulo for existing keys, so new records for a key may land in a different partition from its old ones, and ordering for that key breaks across the change.
  • Removing is not supported at all.

The practical advice is to estimate the peak consumer count you could need over the next few years, add a margin, and prefer a count with many divisors, such as 12, 24 or 48, so that consumers divide evenly.

Messages are limited in size, to about 1 MB by default in Kafka. As with queues, large payloads belong in object storage with a reference in the record.

Designing a distributed message queue

“Design a distributed message queue” is a standard interview question, and working through it is the best check that the pieces above fit together. This section follows the method of in compressed form.

Requirements and size

  • Producers send messages to named topics, and consumers read them.
  • Messages can be read by several independent consumers, and read again.
  • Order is preserved for messages that share a key.
  • The delivery guarantee is configurable: at most once, at least once, or exactly once within the system.
  • Messages are kept for two weeks.
  • No acknowledged message is lost when a server fails.
  • Throughput is high and latency is a few milliseconds.

Take 100,000 messages a second of 1 KB each. That is 100 MB a second, 8.64 TB a day and 121 TB over two weeks, or 363 TB with three replicas. At 10 TB of usable disk for each broker, storage alone calls for about 37 brokers. Repeated consumption and retention point to the log model, not the delete-on-acknowledge model.

The two paths

Writing. The producer looks up the leader for the record’s partition, adds the record to the current batch for that partition, and sends the batch when it is full or the wait expires. The leader appends the batch to its active segment. Followers fetch it. When all in-sync replicas have it, the leader advances the high watermark and acknowledges the producer. If the acknowledgment does not arrive, the producer resends, and the broker uses the producer’s ID and sequence number to drop the duplicate.

Reading. A consumer joins its group and is assigned partitions. For each one it fetches from its committed offset, or from the start or end of the log if it has none. The broker finds the segment through the index and returns records up to the high watermark. The consumer processes them and commits the new offset. If it dies, the coordinator notices missed heartbeats, reassigns its partitions, and the new owner resumes from the last committed offset.

Scaling and failure

  • A broker fails. The controllers choose a new leader for each partition it led, from that partition’s in-sync replicas. Producers and consumers refresh their metadata and continue. No committed record is lost, because a committed record is on every in-sync replica.
  • No in-sync replica is left. The choice is between waiting for one to return, which is unavailability, and promoting a replica that is behind, which loses data. This is the CAP decision of , exposed as a setting. The safe default is to wait.
  • A broker is added. It starts empty. Partition replicas are moved to it gradually, with the copy rate throttled so that the move does not starve live traffic.
  • A topic needs more throughput. Add partitions, accepting that the key-to-partition mapping changes for new records.

Features often asked for

  • Filtering. Consumers that want a subset can filter after fetching, which wastes transfer. To filter in the broker without decoding payloads, give each record a small tag in its metadata and let consumers subscribe by tag.
  • Delayed and scheduled messages. A log delivers in offset order, so a message due in an hour cannot simply sit in the topic. Write it to an internal holding topic for its delay level, such as one minute, ten minutes or one hour, and have a broker-side process move each message to its real topic when it falls due.
  • Retries and dead letters. A consumer that cannot process a record publishes it to a retry topic and moves on. After a set number of attempts it goes to a dead-letter topic.
  • Long retention. Move closed segments to object storage and keep only recent ones on broker disks.

In an interview. The decision that shows most understanding is the first one. Say why you are not using a database for message storage: “the access pattern is append at the tail and scan forward from an offset, so segment files with a sparse index give sequential I/O in both directions, and retention becomes deleting whole files”. Then the rest of the design follows from partitions.

Key points

  • A log is append-only, numbers each record with an offset, and does not delete on read. Consumers are positions in it.
  • That gives many independent readers, replay and exact order within one log.
  • A topic is split into partitions. The key chooses the partition, so order is kept for each key and not across keys.
  • Each partition is replicated with a leader and in-sync followers. A record is committed when all in-sync replicas have it.
  • Three replicas, a minimum of two in sync and acks=all is the standard safe configuration.
  • Logs are fast because they append sequentially, batch end to end and lean on the operating system’s cache.
  • A consumer group gives each partition to one member, so the partition count limits parallelism. Process first, then commit.
  • Watch consumer lag. Choose the partition count and key carefully, because both are hard to change.

Check yourself

A new fraud-detection service needs every payment event from the last 30 days. Why is this easy with a log and impossible with a plain queue?

A log keeps records after they are read, for as long as its retention period. The new service joins as a new consumer group, starts from the oldest offset and reads the history, with no effect on other consumers. A queue deleted each message when its original consumer acknowledged it, so the past no longer exists.

Events for one user must be processed in order, and there are 20 million users. How do you get both order and parallelism?

Use the user ID as the record key. All events for a user hash to the same partition and are read in order by whichever consumer owns it. Different users fall in different partitions, which different consumers process in parallel. Order across users is not preserved and is not needed.

A topic has 6 partitions and its consumer group has 8 consumers, yet lag keeps growing. Why does adding more consumers not help?

Each partition is read by one member of the group, so only 6 of the 8 consumers have work and the other two are idle. More consumers change nothing. The options are to make processing faster, to add partitions, accepting the change in key mapping, or to use a share group if ordering is not required.

A topic has three replicas and producers use acks=all, yet records were lost when a broker failed. What configuration was probably missing?

A minimum number of in-sync replicas. Two followers had fallen behind and left the in-sync set, so “all in-sync replicas” meant the leader alone. Writes were acknowledged with a single copy, and that broker then failed. Setting min.insync.replicas=2 makes the leader refuse writes in that situation.

A consumer reads a batch, commits the offset, then starts processing and crashes. What has happened to those records, and what should the order be?

They are lost to this group: the committed offset says they are done, so the next consumer resumes after them. That is at-most-once delivery. Process first and commit afterward, which gives at-least-once, and make processing idempotent so that the resulting repeats are harmless.

What does log compaction keep, and what kind of topic is it for?

At least the most recent record for every key, removing older records for the same key. It is for topics that represent current state, such as the latest profile of each user or the latest committed offset of each consumer group, where a new reader should be able to rebuild all current values without reading the full history.

In the message queue design, why do consumers pull from brokers instead of brokers pushing to consumers?

With pull, each consumer fetches at the rate it can process, in batches of a size it chooses, and can start from any offset. A slow consumer falls behind without being flooded. With push, the broker would have to track every consumer’s capacity and position, and a slow consumer would be overwhelmed or would need its own flow control.
