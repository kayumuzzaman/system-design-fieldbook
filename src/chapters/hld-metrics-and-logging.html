The problem

The problem. Design the monitoring platform for a company running thousands of services. It collects metrics from every service and host, keeps them for two years, draws dashboards in a second or two, evaluates alert rules and pages the engineer on call, and stores and searches the services’ logs. Many teams share it, and none of them may be able to break it for the others.

covered what to measure and how to read it: counters, gauges and histograms, structured logs, and alerts on symptoms by burn rate. This chapter designs the system that receives all of it. Its workload is the time-series shape of at the scale of a whole company: a steady flood of small appends, read rarely and mostly as summaries. Two things make it more than a database. It must stay up when the systems it watches are down, since that is when it is needed. And its cost grows with every label a developer adds, so it has to defend itself. In the terms of , this is counting a stream: millions of samples a second, stored and queried cheaply.

Step 2: Estimates

series: 10,000 targets × 1,000 series = 10 million active series
samples: 10 million ÷ 15 s ≈ 667,000 a second
storage: 667,000 × 2.13 bytes a sample (measured below) × 86,400 s ≈ 123 GB a day; 15 days ≈ 1.84 TB
rollups, four values of 2.13 bytes each: 10 million × (288 a day × 90 days + 24 a day × 730 days) × 8.5 bytes ≈ 3.7 TB
logs: 2 TB a day ÷ 6.3 (compression, measured below) × 30 days ≈ 9.5 TB; an index of every word would add about 27 TB

Three conclusions follow (). The samples are cheap once compressed: two years of metrics fit in a few terabytes of object storage. The rate is not: 667,000 appends a second must be spread across machines by series. And the logs dwarf the metrics, so the decision about how to index them is the largest cost decision in the system.

A series is identified by its name and all its labels, so each new combination of label values is a new series, with its own index entries and its own chunk in memory. The number of series, not the number of samples, is what exhausts an ingester, which is why limits count series ().

Step 5: The design

Collection is by pull or by push. To scrape is to fetch a target’s current values over HTTP on a fixed schedule, the pull model: the platform holds the list of targets, and a target that does not answer is itself a signal. Push suits short-lived jobs and devices that cannot be reached, and it is how collectors forward what they gathered. Scraping has one trap that the simulation measured: if 10,000 targets are all scraped at the start of each 15-second interval, the busiest second receives 15 times the average. Prometheus gives each target a fixed offset within the interval, derived from a hash of the target, and with offsets chosen that way the busiest second received 1.06 times the average.

Distributors check limits and send each series to three ingesters, picked by a hash of its labels on a ring (), so that one ingester holds all of a series and losing one loses nothing.

Step 6a: Storing a sample in two bytes

A sample is a 64-bit time and a 64-bit value: 16 bytes stored plainly, and about 0.9 TB a day at this scale. Consecutive samples of one series are much alike, and the compression introduced by Facebook’s Gorilla exploits that: each time is stored as the change in the gap since the last sample, and each value as the bits that differ from the last value.

The chapter’s code implements the scheme and measured it on two-hour chunks of 480 samples, for five kinds of series. Every chunk decoded to exactly the samples that went in.

Two lessons sit in the table. Values that are whole numbers or repeat compress well, and arbitrary fractions hardly at all, so a gauge exported as a ratio costs three times what the same quantity costs as two counters. And timestamps that land exactly on the schedule cost one bit, while a few milliseconds of jitter cost nine, so collectors that keep to the schedule save storage. Facebook reported 1.37 bytes a sample on its own data, and the Prometheus documentation says 1 to 2.

Ingesters keep the open chunk of every series in memory and append each batch to a write-ahead log first (), so a crashed ingester replays its log and loses nothing it acknowledged. Every two hours they cut a block: the chunks of that period, plus an index from each label value to the sorted ids of the series that have it. A query for service="feed", status="500" intersects two such lists and reads only the chunks of the series in both. Blocks go to object storage, where a compactor later merges small blocks into large ones and writes the downsampled versions.

Step 6b: Downsampling, and what survives it

Downsampling replaces the samples of a period with a few numbers that summarize it, so that long ranges can be read without reading every sample. A day of one series is 5,760 samples at 15 seconds, 288 five-minute rollups or 24 hourly ones: a query over a year reads hourly rollups, 8,760 rows per series instead of 2.1 million samples. Each rollup stores the minimum, maximum, sum and count, because those combine again exactly: the test checked that hourly rollups built from five-minute rollups equal hourly rollups built from the raw samples. An average alone would not, and a maximum hidden in an average is the spike someone will later look for. The same reasoning as the rollups of ad clicks applies: store what adds up.

Percentiles do not add up. A dashboard showing hourly latency from per-minute 99th percentiles has to combine them somehow, and every way is wrong.

In the simulated hour, 120,000 requests with five slow minutes, the true 99th percentile was 864 ms. The average of the 60 per-minute percentiles was 230 ms, a quarter of the truth; the largest was 1,408 ms. Adding the 60 minutes’ histograms bucket by bucket and reading the percentile from the total gave 877 ms, off only by the width of the bucket it fell in (). So rollups of latency keep histogram buckets, and percentiles are computed at query time from whatever range the query covers.

Queriers split a range by age: the last two hours from the ingesters, older data from blocks in object storage, at the coarsest resolution that still gives the dashboard enough points. Results for completed intervals never change, so they are cached by query and interval, and a dashboard refreshed every minute recomputes only the newest step.

Step 6c: Alerts that page for the right reasons

A rule evaluator runs each rule’s query every 15 seconds. When the condition holds, an alert is pending; if the rule has a “for” duration, it fires only once the condition has held that long without a break. The rule language makes it easy to write rules that page too often, or never. The simulation built a day of a service’s 99th-percentile latency: about 120 ms, with brief spikes above 300 ms a few dozen times a day, and one real incident from 14:00 to 14:20 at about 450 ms, during which one sample in ten dipped below 300.

  • Every sample above 300 ms paged 33 times: 23 false alarms from spikes, and 10 pages during the incident itself, because each dip ended the alert and the next sample started a new one. That is flapping: an alert that fires and resolves repeatedly while the underlying problem continues.
  • Above 300 ms for 1 minute removed every false alarm, but still paged 5 times during the incident, first after 90 seconds.
  • Above 300 ms for 5 minutes never paged at all. Each dip below the threshold reset the pending timer, and no run of 20 samples above it ever happened. A twenty-minute incident went unreported.
  • The 5-minute average above 300 ms paged once, 165 seconds into the incident, and never otherwise.

The lesson is to put the smoothing in the query, over a window, rather than in a timer that any single sample can reset. Better still is the burn-rate alert of , which asks how fast the error budget is being spent over two windows at once. Firing alerts go to an alert manager that groups related alerts into one notification, removes repeats, routes by team and severity, and honors silences during planned work. Two rule evaluators run the same rules for safety, and the alert manager removes the duplicates they send.

In production. A monitoring system that is down sends no alerts, which looks exactly like a healthy system. Teams therefore run an alert that always fires, sent to an outside service that pages when it stops arriving, and watch the platform from a second, independent one.

Step 6d: Logs

Logs are the largest data in the system, and the main choice is what to index. A search engine indexes every word (), so any text query is a lookup. The alternative, used by Grafana Loki, indexes only a few labels per stream, such as service and level, keeps the lines in compressed chunks in object storage, and scans the chunks whose labels and time range match. The chapter measured both on 200,000 synthetic JSON log lines, 43.3 MB as written.

Compressed in chunks of about 256 KB per stream, the lines took 6.9 MB, 6.3 times smaller. An index of every word took 19.6 MB, almost three times the compressed logs, because most of its 486,891 terms were trace ids and user ids that occur once. The label index took 12.1 KB. In one run, finding one trace id took 0.1 ms with the word index, 13.3 ms by decompressing and scanning one service’s 3.6 MB of chunks, and 194 ms by scanning everything.

At 2 TB a day the difference is about 27 TB of index for a month, against 9.5 TB for the logs themselves. Scanning is slower, but it parallelizes across chunks, and most searches name a service and a time range, which is what the label index narrows. Logs that need instant search by many fields, such as audit and security logs, justify a full index on a smaller volume. Older logs move to cheaper storage classes (), and routine lines are sampled or turned into metrics before they are ever stored.

At ten times the scale, 100 million series and 6.7 million samples a second, the design is the same with more distributors and ingesters, about 18 TB for 15 days of samples, and collectors in every cluster. The pressure moves to queries: dashboards that once read thousands of series now read millions, so queries are split by time and by series across queriers. At a hundred times, the index of a block and the series held in memory become the limit, and the answer is fewer series rather than more machines: aggregating at the collector, dropping labels nobody queries and sampling logs.

In the real world

  • Facebook’s Gorilla (VLDB 2015) introduced the compression used here: about 96% of timestamps stored in a single bit and about 51% of values identical to the previous one, for an average of 1.37 bytes a point against 16, in two-hour blocks.
  • Prometheus’s documentation (checked October 2026) describes two-hour blocks compacted in the background, a write-ahead log in 128 MB segments, a default retention of 15 days, and “an average of only 1-2 bytes per sample”. Its source code gives each target a scrape offset from a hash of the target.
  • In Prometheus, an alert with a for clause is pending until the condition has held for that long; keep_firing_for keeps it firing for a while after the condition clears, against flapping.
  • Grafana Loki indexes only labels, not the contents of log lines, and stores compressed chunks in object storage, which its documentation credits for its lower cost (checked October 2026). Grafana Mimir’s stateless distributors validate samples, enforce limits per tenant and shard series across ingesters.
  • Google’s Monarch (VLDB 2020) is a globally distributed, in-memory time-series database that ingests terabytes of data a second and serves millions of queries a second.

Key points

  • The platform is write-heavy: millions of samples a second, sharded by series and replicated three ways.
  • Stored as changes in gaps and differing bits, a sample costs one or two bytes; fractions and jitter cost more.
  • Recent data lives in ingesters’ memory with a write-ahead log; two-hour blocks with a label index go to object storage.
  • Rollups keep minimum, maximum, sum, count and histogram buckets, because those combine; percentiles never do.
  • Smooth alert conditions over a window. A plain threshold flaps, and a long “for” can miss a real incident.
  • Indexing every word of the logs costs more than the logs; indexing labels and scanning chunks is the cheap default.
  • Limits per team, and an outside check that the monitoring itself is alive.

Check yourself

Why does adding a label for the user id break a metrics system, when the number of samples per second hardly changes?

Every distinct combination of label values is a separate series, with its own index entries and its own open chunk in an ingester’s memory. A million users turn one series into a million. Memory and index size grow with series, not samples. Per-tenant series limits refuse such pushes, and user ids belong in logs and traces.

A sample whose timestamp is 2 ms late costs 9 bits instead of 1 for its time. Why?

Times are stored as the change in the gap between samples. Exactly on schedule, the gap is unchanged and costs a single zero bit. Two milliseconds late, the gap changed by 2, which needs a short code: a 2-bit prefix and 7 bits of value.

Why do rollups store the minimum, maximum, sum and count rather than the average?

Because those four combine exactly into coarser rollups: the minimum of minimums, the maximum of maximums, and added sums and counts, from which the average follows. Averages of averages are wrong when counts differ, and an average hides the spike the maximum keeps.

Per-minute 99th percentiles are stored. How should a dashboard show the hourly 99th percentile?

It cannot, correctly: averaging them gave 230 ms for a true 864 ms in the chapter’s test. Store histogram buckets per minute instead, add them for the hour, and read the percentile from the total, which gave 877 ms.

An alert “p99 above 300 ms for 5 minutes” stayed silent through a 20-minute incident. How?

The pending timer restarts whenever one evaluation falls below the threshold, and during the incident about one sample in ten dipped to around 280 ms. No unbroken 5-minute run ever happened. A condition on the 5-minute average, or a burn rate, does not have this weakness.

Why is a full-text index of all logs usually the wrong default?

Because it can be larger than the logs: in the test, 19.6 MB of index for 6.9 MB of compressed logs, mostly unique ids that each appear once. Most searches name a service and a time range, so a label index plus scanning the matching compressed chunks answers them in milliseconds to seconds at a fraction of the storage.

How do you know the monitoring system is working, given that it cannot alert about its own death?

Run an alert that always fires and send it to an independent outside service, which pages when the alert stops arriving. Watch the platform’s own health from a second monitoring system that does not share its failure modes.
