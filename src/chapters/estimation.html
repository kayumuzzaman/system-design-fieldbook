Why a rough number is enough

“Ten million users” does not tell you what to build. It could mean ten million people who each load one page a month, or ten million who stream video all evening. The first fits on a single modest server. The second needs a global delivery network. You find out which one you have by turning the user count into the three quantities machines care about: operations per second, bytes stored, and bytes sent per second.

This is called back-of-the-envelope estimation, after the habit of doing it on whatever paper is nearby. The aim is an order of magnitude, a power of ten, and nothing finer. Whether you will handle 20 writes per second or 30 changes nothing. Whether it is 20 or 20,000 changes almost every decision, and a two-minute calculation tells you which.

An estimate earns its place when it settles a question such as these:

  • Does all the data fit on one machine, or must it be split across several?
  • Can the database keep up with the write rate by itself?
  • Does the hot data fit in memory, so that a cache is practical?
  • Is the outbound traffic small enough to serve from our own servers?
  • Roughly what will this cost per month?

The only arithmetic you need

Estimation is multiplication and division of round numbers. Three sets of facts make it quick enough to do in your head.

KB or KiB?Strictly, 1 KB is 1,000 bytes and 1 KiB (kibibyte) is 1,024. Memory is usually sold in the binary units and disks and networks in the decimal ones. The gap is 2.4% at the kilobyte and about 10% at the terabyte. For estimation, treat them as the same and move on.

To multiply, add the exponents: a million users times a thousand bytes each is 106 × 103 = 109 bytes, or 1 GB. To divide, subtract them. Keep the leading digits small and round freely. 86,400 becomes 105, and 365 becomes 400 when you only need the magnitude.

One trap deserves a warning. Storage is measured in bytes and network speed in bits per second, and a byte is eight bits. A “1 gigabit” link moves 125 megabytes per second, not 1,000.

Seconds in a day

Product numbers arrive per day or per month. Machines are sized per second. The conversion factor is worth memorizing.

PeriodSecondsRound figure
1 day86,400about 105
1 month (30 days)2,592,000about 2.5 million
1 year31,536,000about 30 million

From the first row comes the most useful shortcut in this chapter: one million events per day is about 12 per second. So a billion per day is about 12,000 per second, and 100,000 per day is about one per second. In the other direction, one event per second is 86,400 a day, or about 2.6 million a month.

Average and peak

Dividing a daily total by 86,400 gives the average rate, as if traffic were spread evenly over 24 hours. It never is. People are awake at the same time, and a system sized for the average fails every evening. Multiply by a peak factor to get the rate you must actually survive. For a service used through the day in one region, 2 to 3 times the average is a common planning figure. A ticket sale or a breaking news alert can be ten times or more, and those need their own estimate.

The second ingredient is the size of one item. You will not know it exactly at design time, and you do not need to. These figures are close enough for planning:

The table shows something that shapes many architectures: sizes span seven orders of magnitude. One minute of video outweighs the metadata rows of forty thousand users. Whenever a system handles media, the media dominates storage and bandwidth and the rest is rounding error. That is why media goes to object storage and a CDN, and why the database that holds everything else stays small for much longer than people expect.

Two billion is not a large numberA signed 32-bit integer tops out at 2,147,483,647. A busy table that numbers its rows with one will run out, and production outages have been caused by exactly that. How long things take

The third ingredient is time. Computers work across a range of speeds so wide that intuition fails, so engineers keep a short list of reference times. A version of this list was made popular by Jeff Dean of Google in a 2009 talk. Hardware has moved since, mainly because solid-state drives replaced spinning disks, and the proportions still hold.

Four lessons from the figure come up again and again:

  • Memory is about a thousand times faster than a fast disk. This is the whole case for caching, and for databases keeping their hot data in RAM.
  • A network call inside a data center costs about as much as several disk reads. Splitting one program into ten services that call each other adds a cost that was zero before. Twenty such calls in sequence add about 10 ms.
  • Crossing the world costs as much as a million memory reads. Nothing in software fixes this, because it is set by the speed of light. Only placing data nearer the user does.
  • Spinning disks are slow at jumping around and fine at reading straight through. Much of storage-engine design, covered in , comes from turning random access into sequential access.

What one machine can do

To turn a rate into a number of servers you need a rough idea of what a single one handles. This is the least reliable part of any estimate, because it depends on what each request does. Treat the figures below as starting assumptions to be replaced by measurement.

Common mistake. Quoting a benchmark as if it were your capacity. “PostgreSQL does 50,000 queries per second” is true of some query on some machine. Your query joins four tables and returns 200 rows. Use round planning figures to choose an architecture, then load test the real thing before you promise anything. See .

The method: five quantities

Every estimate in this book produces the same five numbers, in the same order, because each one feeds the next.

  1. Traffic. Requests per second, average and peak, with reads and writes counted separately.
  2. Storage. New bytes per day, then the total over the period you must keep data, times the number of copies.
  3. Bandwidth. Bytes per second in and out, which is traffic times the size of each request or response.
  4. Memory. How much hot data would have to sit in RAM for a cache to be effective.
  5. Machines. Peak traffic divided by what one server handles, plus spare capacity for failures.

A worked example: the photo app

Here is the full estimate for the photo-sharing app from Chapter 1. Start by writing down the assumptions, because they are the part other people can challenge.

AssumptionValue
Daily active users10 million
Photos viewed per user per day50
Photos uploaded per user per day0.2 (one upload every five days)
Stored per upload: the original plus resized versions2.5 MB
Sent per view: the feed-sized version200 KB
Metadata per photo1 KB
Peak factor3
Copies kept of every stored byte3
Retention5 years

1. Traffic

uploads 10,000,000 × 0.2 = 2,000,000 per day ÷ 86,400 ≈ 23 per second peak ≈ 70 views 10,000,000 × 50 = 500,000,000 per day ÷ 86,400 ≈ 5,800 per second peak ≈ 17,400 ratio 500,000,000 ÷ 2,000,000 = 250 reads for every write

A view of a photo is a request for an image file. The calls to our own API are fewer. If the feed loads ten photos per page, 50 views is five page loads per user per day:

feed API 10,000,000 × 5 = 50,000,000 per day ÷ 86,400 ≈ 580 per second peak ≈ 1,740

2. Storage

images 2,000,000 × 2.5 MB = 5 TB per day 5 TB × 365 ≈ 1.8 PB per year × 3 copies ≈ 5.5 PB per year over 5 years ≈ 27 PB metadata 2,000,000 × 1 KB = 2 GB per day ≈ 730 GB per year ≈ 3.7 TB over 5 years photos 2,000,000 × 365 × 5 ≈ 3.65 billion rows after 5 years

3. Bandwidth

in 23 uploads/s × 2 MB ≈ 46 MB/s ≈ 370 Mbit/s out 5,800 views/s × 200 KB ≈ 1.16 GB/s ≈ 9.3 Gbit/s peak ≈ 28 Gbit/s

4. Memory

Requests are rarely spread evenly over the data. A small share of items draws most of the attention, and a common assumption is that 20% of the items receive 80% of the requests. Suppose 100 million distinct photos are viewed on a given day. Caching the metadata for the most popular fifth of them takes:

cache 100,000,000 × 20% × 1 KB = 20 GB

That fits in the memory of one server, with room to spare.

5. Machines

Assume one application server handles 1,000 feed requests per second. The peak of 1,740 needs two. Running exactly two leaves nothing for a server failing or a deployment in progress, so plan for four and expect them to sit below half load most of the day. explains why that spare capacity is necessary and not waste.

What the numbers decide

The estimate took a few minutes. Look at how much of the architecture it settles.

The numberWhat it tells you
250 reads per writeOptimize the read path. Caches, replicas and precomputed feeds pay off, and the write path can stay simple.
23 uploads per second, 70 at peakOne relational database handles the write rate easily. Sharding for write volume is not needed.
3.7 TB of metadata in 5 yearsFits on one large database server. It will need attention eventually, and not on day one.
3.65 billion photo rowsThe photo ID must be 64-bit. Queries on this table must use indexes, since scanning it is never an option.
27 PB of imagesFar beyond any single machine or any database. Use object storage, and think about cheaper storage tiers for old photos, since this is where the money goes.
9 Gbit/s outbound, 28 at peakDo not serve images from application servers. A CDN is required, both for speed and to carry the traffic.
20 GB of hot metadataA cache is cheap and practical.
1,740 API requests per second at peakA handful of stateless application servers behind a load balancer.

Just as useful is what the numbers say you can ignore. Nothing here calls for a distributed database, a fleet of hundreds of servers or a custom storage engine. The hard parts of this system are storing and delivering images cheaply and building the feed quickly. Everything else is ordinary, and should be built in the most ordinary way available.

In an interview. Say your assumptions aloud and round hard. “Ten million daily users, each viewing about fifty photos, so five hundred million views a day, which is about six thousand a second” takes fifteen seconds and shows the skill being tested. Then use the result: “so this is read-heavy and bandwidth-heavy, and I’ll put a CDN in front of the images.” An estimate that does not change the design was not worth doing.

Mistakes to avoid

  • False precision. Writing 5,787.04 requests per second claims knowledge you do not have. The inputs are guesses, so the output is good to one significant digit at best. Say “about 6,000”.
  • Sizing for the average. The average is what you pay for over a month. The peak is what takes you down. Always compute both.
  • Mixing bits and bytes. Off by a factor of eight. Write the unit beside every number.
  • Forgetting the copies. Stored data is replicated, backed up and indexed. Raw size times three is a reasonable floor for what you will pay for.
  • Forgetting growth. A system designed for exactly today’s load is full on the day it launches. Estimate for where you expect to be in a year or two, and state the growth rate you assumed.
  • Estimating what does not matter. If the metadata is a thousandth of the image data, do not spend time refining the metadata estimate. Find the dominant term and get that one right.
  • Hiding the assumptions. An estimate is only as good as its inputs. List them first so that someone who knows better can correct one, and the correction flows through.

In production. At work you often have real data, so use it. Last month’s request logs beat any assumption about user behavior. Estimation still matters there for the things you cannot measure yet: a feature that does not exist, a tenfold growth target, or the cost of a migration. A good habit is to write the estimate in the design document and, after launch, compare it with what happened. That is how the planning figures in your head get better.

Key points

  • Estimation aims for the right power of ten. That is enough to choose between kinds of architecture.
  • A day has about 105 seconds, so a million events per day is about 12 per second.
  • Always compute the peak as well as the average, and keep reads and writes separate.
  • Media sizes are thousands of times larger than metadata. Find the dominant term first.
  • Memory is about a thousand times faster than a fast disk, and a trip across the world costs about 150 ms that no software can remove.
  • Produce the same five numbers every time: traffic, storage, bandwidth, memory, machines. Then say what each one decides.

Check yourself

A service receives 250 million requests per day. Roughly how many per second on average, and what would you plan for at peak?

One million per day is about 12 per second, so 250 million is about 3,000 per second (2,894 exactly). With a peak factor of 3, plan for roughly 9,000 per second.

A chat app has 50 million daily users who each send 40 messages of about 200 bytes. How much new message data per day, before replication?

50,000,000 × 40 = 2 billion messages per day. At 200 bytes each that is 400 GB per day, or about 146 TB per year. Text is small, and at this scale it still adds up to something that needs a plan.

Your link is rated at 1 Gbit/s. A colleague says it can serve 1,000 downloads per second of a 1 MB file. Is that right?

No. 1 Gbit/s is 125 MB per second, so the link can carry at most about 125 such downloads per second. The colleague mixed up bits and bytes.

In the photo app, which single assumption would change the storage cost the most if it were wrong by a factor of two?

Anything in the image chain: uploads per user, bytes stored per upload, or the number of copies. Storage is the product of those, so doubling any one doubles the 27 PB. The metadata size could be wrong by ten times and the total would barely move.

Why is “one server handles 1,000 requests per second” the weakest number in the estimate?

Because it depends on what each request does, on the hardware and on the code, none of which exist yet. The other inputs are counts and sizes that can be reasoned about. This one can only be confirmed by measuring the real system under load.
