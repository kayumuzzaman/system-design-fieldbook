The problem

The problem. Design a reservation system that sells hotel rooms and concert tickets. Hotel guests search by city and dates, pick a room type and book a stay of several nights. Concert fans buy up to four seats when a sale opens, and for the biggest acts a hundred thousand people arrive in the first minute for twenty thousand seats. Nothing may be sold twice, and a big sale must neither collapse nor reward bots.

The low-level design in already sells cinema seats correctly inside one service: a hold is one conditional write, unpaid holds expire, and payments are idempotent. Three things change at this scale. A hotel stay is a set of nights, and a reservation must take all of them or none. A hotel can sell more rooms than it has, on purpose. And a big sale concentrates so much demand on so little inventory that the bottleneck moves from correctness to survival: the same rows, the same service, the same minute. In the terms of , this is contended inventory: never selling a seat twice while a crowd tries at once.

hotel reservations: 1 million rooms × 70% ÷ 3 nights ≈ 233,000 a day ≈ 2.7 a second
hotel searches: about 100 for each reservation ≈ 270 a second, around 1,000 at the peak
inventory rows: 5,000 hotels × 5 room types × 365 nights ≈ 9.1 million rows, under 1 GB
a big sale: 100,000 people, 80% of them within the first minute: about 1,300 arrivals a second against a booking service that completes 200 orders a second
with a waiting room admitting 90% of that, 180 a second: 20,000 tickets last 20,000 ÷ 180 ≈ 111 s

The two halves are opposites (). Hotels are small: a few reservations a second, a database under a gigabyte, and the difficulty is in making each reservation right. Sales are short and violent: for two minutes the load is many times what the booking service can do, and it falls on a few rows. Nobody sizes a system for two minutes of a hundred times normal load; the design instead decides how many people may try at once.

A hotel row counts rooms, not room numbers: guests reserve “a double for three nights”, and the hotel assigns a numbered room on arrival. Counting is what makes overbooking possible and the inventory small. Seats in a stadium are numbered, so a sale keeps one row per seat, and a count per part of a section for the fast path of “any two seats in section A”.

Step 5: The design

Search never touches the rows that bookings write. It reads a cache of availability that follows the database a few seconds behind, so the “2 left” on a search result can be wrong by the time a guest clicks. That is acceptable because the booking path checks again where it counts.

Booking is one write. The booking service turns a request into a conditional update over every night or seat the order needs, inside one transaction, records the hold, and only then asks for payment. A confirmed payment turns the hold into a reservation; a declined one or an expired hold gives the inventory back, as works through case by case. Events about confirmed reservations go out through an outbox () to email and to the hotels’ own systems.

The waiting room sits at the edge, in front of the booking service, and is switched on for sales expected to exceed capacity. It holds the crowd, hands out places, admits people at a set rate, and tells everyone still in line when the event is sold out.

Step 6a: A stay is several nights

A guest who books three nights needs a room on each, and a room free on two of the three is no use. Inventory is counted per night, so a reservation is a write to several rows, and the question is what happens between those writes while other guests book. The chapter raced 600 guests, each wanting one to four nights, against a hotel with 50 rooms of one type over a week, allowed to sell 52 a night. Every call to the store took 1 to 3 ms, and a deterministic clock interleaved the guests the same way on every run.

export async function reserve(store: InventoryStore, keys: string[], how: Strategy): Promise {
  switch (how) {
    case "read, then write":
      for (const k of keys) {
        const row = await store.read(k);
        if (row.reserved + 1 > store.limit(row)) return false;   // earlier nights stay written
        await store.write(k, row.reserved + 1);                  // someone may have written in between
      }
      return true;
    case "night by night":
      for (const k of keys) if (!(await store.reserveOne(k))) return false;
      return true;
    case "night by night, undo on failure": {
      const done: string[] = [];
      for (const k of keys) {
        if (!(await store.reserveOne(k))) { for (const d of done) await store.releaseOne(d); return false; }
        done.push(k);
      }
      return true;
    }
    case "all nights at once":
      return store.reserveAll(keys);
  }
}

Giving the nights back on failure, a small saga (), made the counts right in the end, yet sold only 303 room-nights, because nights held for a few milliseconds by guests who would later give them up turned others away. One transaction over all the nights sold 349 of the 364 possible.

BEGIN;
UPDATE room_inventory
   SET reserved = reserved + 1
 WHERE hotel_id = $1 AND room_type = $2
   AND night >= $3 AND night < $3 + $4                   -- the nights of the stay
   AND reserved + 1 <= floor(total * (1 + overbooking));
-- if fewer than $4 rows changed, a night was full: ROLLBACK
INSERT INTO reservations (id, hotel_id, room_type, checkin, nights, status, hold_ends)
VALUES ($5, $1, $2, $3, $4, 'held', now() + interval '10 minutes');   -- the id is the primary key
COMMIT;

The insert of the reservation, keyed by the id the client made, is what makes the request idempotent: a retry hits the primary key, rolls back, and reads the reservation that exists. The inventory rows of one hotel are all on one database, so this is an ordinary local transaction; partitioning by hotel keeps it that way at any scale (). A design that put each night, or each room type, in a different service would need a distributed transaction for every booking, which is the strongest argument for keeping them together.

Step 6b: Selling rooms that do not exist

Some guests cancel at the last moment and some never arrive. A hotel that sells exactly its rooms will have some empty every night. Overbooking is accepting more reservations than there are rooms, in the expectation that enough of them will not arrive, and paying for the nights when too many do, usually by finding the guest a room in another hotel. In the design it is one number in the limit of the conditional update.

How far to go is arithmetic. If each reservation arrives with probability p, the number who arrive out of n follows a binomial distribution, and the expected empty rooms and turned-away guests follow from it exactly. The chapter computed both for a hotel of 100 rooms where 92% of reservations arrive, and checked one point against 100,000 simulated nights.

Selling exactly 100 leaves 8 rooms empty on an average night. Five percent more cuts that to 3.5, at the cost of 0.11 guests without a room per night, which happens on 7% of nights. Ten percent leaves 0.65 rooms empty and turns away 1.85 guests a night, on 62% of nights. Which is right depends on the price of an empty room against the cost of a guest sent elsewhere, which includes their goodwill, and the real arrival rate differs by hotel, season and how the room was sold; a revenue team sets the number per hotel and night, and the inventory row stores it.

Step 6c: A sale that everyone wants

A big sale fails in two places: the rows that count the seats, and the service in front of them.

One row for a whole section

A hot row is a single database row that a large share of all transactions must change, so that they queue for its lock. “Seats left in section A” is one: every buyer of section A updates it, each transaction holds the row’s lock until it commits, and the commits happen one after another. The chapter measured it on PostgreSQL 16 with pgbench, 32 clients, each transaction taking one ticket and recording a hold.

One row allowed 1,050 transactions a second at 30 ms each, most of it waiting for the lock. The same million tickets split over 4 rows allowed 3,141, over 16 rows 5,428, and over 64 rows 7,540, by which point the two cores of the test machine were the limit rather than the locks. Splitting a counter like this works because a buyer wants “two seats in section A”, not a particular part of it: the request picks a part at random and takes from it. The cost appears at the end of a sale, when some parts are empty and others are not, and a request that finds its part empty must try another; SELECT … FOR UPDATE SKIP LOCKED () lets it take any free seat without waiting for the ones others are locking.

A line in front of the sale

Fixing the rows is not enough, because a crowd breaks the service before the database. The chapter simulated a sale of 20,000 tickets to 100,000 people, of whom 30% are waiting on the page at the opening, half arrive within the first minute and the rest over the next few minutes, plus 500 scripted sessions that retry five times a second. The booking service completes 200 orders a second. A buyer’s app gives up on a request after 10 seconds and tries again a few seconds later, up to ten times; a hold whose buyer has gone lasts 10 minutes. Three designs were compared.

Without protection the sale collapsed in its first ten seconds. Requests arrived several times faster than the service could finish them, the line inside the service grew past the ten seconds any buyer would wait, and from then on the service worked only on requests whose buyers had already given up and retried, adding more to the line. Tickets went into holds for people who were no longer there, to come back ten minutes later, by which time everyone had stopped trying. This is the metastable failure of , with a retry storm feeding it.

Dropping requests that cannot finish before their buyer gives up, the load shedding of , restored the sale: every ticket sold in 100 seconds and no work was wasted. But the experience was a lottery decided by retrying: 711,000 orders were sent, 44,344 people gave up without ever hearing whether tickets were left, and the scripts, retrying five times a second, took 1,877 tickets.

A waiting room is a queue in front of a service that holds arriving users, gives each a place, and admits them at a rate the service can take, telling the rest when there is nothing left. It turns the crowd into a line before it reaches anything that can break. In the simulation it admitted 180 a second and sold every ticket in 111 seconds, slightly slower than shedding at full speed, with every person told an answer and 19,910 orders sent in all. People who were on the page before the opening were given places in random order, so arriving at 09:59:59 was no better than at 09:45, and a script got one place for each account, as a person does; the scripts’ 50 accounts got 120 tickets at four per order.

The rest of fairness is identity. A waiting room that gives a place per browser can be flooded by browsers; one that gives a place per verified account, with a limit per order, makes a script’s advantage cost it accounts. The admission token, signed by the waiting room with a short expiry, is what lets the booking service tell an admitted buyer from one who skipped the line. And the waiting room itself must be cheap to serve: it is mostly static pages and a counter, run at the edge (), so it is not the next thing to fall over.

Hotels grow gently: ten times the hotels is ten times the rows, still partitioned by hotel with every stay inside one partition. Sales grow by events, not by size: a tour selling many stadiums at once is many separate sales, each with its own line and its own inventory, which can run on separate shards. What does not scale is a single event with a hundred times the demand, and there the answer is not capacity but policy: registration before the sale, a lottery for the right to buy, or more dates.

In the real world

  • Ticketmaster wrote, in a post reported on 17 November 2022, that the presale for Taylor Swift’s Eras Tour produced 3.5 billion system requests, four times its previous peak, after 3.5 million people had registered for its Verified Fan program; the public sale was then canceled because of “extraordinarily high demands on ticketing systems and insufficient remaining ticket inventory”.
  • Cloudflare’s Waiting Room (documentation as of October 2026) offers first-in-first-out and random ways to let users out of the line, the second choosing visitors at random, plus modes that let everyone through or no one.

In an interview. Ask which half matters: hotels or big sales. For hotels, model inventory per room type and night, show the one-statement reservation over all nights, mention the client’s reservation id for retries, and price overbooking with the binomial argument. For sales, say early that the database is not the first thing to fail: introduce a waiting room with an admission rate and tokens, then split hot counters, and finish with fairness and bots.

Key points

  • Count hotel inventory per room type and night; a stay is a write to several rows that must succeed together.
  • Reserve all nights in one conditional update in one transaction, with an id made by the client for retries.
  • Overbooking is a number in the limit, chosen from the arrival rate and the costs of an empty room and a turned-away guest.
  • A popular section’s counter is a hot row: split it over several rows, or take individual seats with SKIP LOCKED.
  • Without protection, a big sale collapses into work for people who have left. Shedding stale requests saves the sale; a waiting room also makes it orderly.
  • Fairness comes from identity and order: one place per verified account, random order for early arrivals, tokens checked at booking.

Check yourself

Why does reserving each night with its own conditional update still go wrong?

No night is oversold, but a guest whose third night is full keeps the first two. Those nights are counted as taken and belong to nobody; in the test the hotel looked full while only 165 of 364 room-nights were booked.

What makes the reservation request safe to retry?

The client makes the reservation id before the first attempt, and the reservation is inserted with that id as its primary key in the same transaction as the inventory update. A retry fails on the key, rolls back, and returns the reservation that exists.

A hotel of 100 rooms sees 92% of reservations arrive. Why does overbooking by 10% turn guests away on most nights, though 110 × 0.92 is about 101?

101 is the average; the number who arrive varies around it, and any night with more than 100 arrivals turns someone away. With a binomial spread of about three either side, that happens on 62% of nights.

Why does splitting one inventory row into 16 raise throughput?

Each transaction holds its row’s lock until it commits, so transactions on one row run one at a time. Spread over 16 rows, up to 16 run side by side; in the test throughput went from 1,050 to 5,428 a second.

Without a waiting room, why did the service sell almost nothing although it worked at full speed?

Its internal line grew longer than any buyer would wait, so it processed requests whose buyers had already given up, and held tickets for them. The retries joined the end of the line and met the same fate.

Dropping stale requests sold every ticket faster than the waiting room. Why use a waiting room at all?

Because with shedding alone the outcome is decided by retrying: hundreds of thousands of requests, tens of thousands of people who never got an answer, and scripts that retry fastest take the most. A waiting room gives everyone a place and an answer, and limits scripts to one place per account.

What does the admission token protect against?

Buyers or scripts calling the booking service directly and skipping the line. The token, signed by the waiting room and short-lived, proves the holder was admitted; the booking service refuses holds without one while the sale is protected.
