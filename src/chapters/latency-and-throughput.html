Two measures of speed

Picture a road between two towns. One question you can ask is how long a single car takes to drive it. Another is how many cars arrive at the far end every hour. The two are related, and they are different measurements with different units. A narrow mountain road may be quick for the one car that has it to itself and still carry very few cars per hour.

Systems have the same two measures.

  • Latency is the time one operation takes, measured in milliseconds. When the operation is a whole request as the client experiences it, from sending to receiving the answer, the more precise term is response time. Most people use the two words interchangeably, and so does this book unless the difference matters.
  • Throughput is the amount of work completed per unit of time: requests per second, messages per second, megabytes per second.

A third word causes confusion. Bandwidth is the maximum rate a link can carry, the number of lanes on the road. Throughput is what actually gets through, and it can never exceed bandwidth. More lanes do not shorten the drive for a lone car, and more bandwidth does not reduce the latency of a small request.

What response time is made of

A response time is a sum, and the first step in improving one is to find its largest part.

The previous chapter dealt with the two gray segments. This chapter is mostly about the other two, and about the fact that the service time of one component contains the full response times of everything it calls.

Why the average misleads

Ask how fast a service is and the usual answer is an average: “about 120 milliseconds”. That number is nearly useless, and understanding why is one of the more valuable things in this chapter.

Response times are not clustered evenly around a middle value. Most requests are quick, and a minority take much longer because they hit a cache miss, a garbage-collection pause, a lock held by another request, or a retried network packet. Plotted as a histogram, the shape has a tall hump on the left and a long thin tail to the right.

The mean in is 120 ms. It is pulled to the right by the tail, so most requests are faster than it, and it says nothing about how bad the slow ones are. Two services with the same mean can feel completely different.

Percentiles describe the shape directly. Sort all the response times from fastest to slowest. The value a given percentage of the way along is that percentile.

The high percentiles are called the tail latency, and there are three reasons to care about a slow 1%.

First, 1% of requests is far more than 1% of users. Someone who loads 100 pages in a session will, on average, meet the p99 once. Second, the slowest requests often belong to your most valuable users, because the customer with the most data, the longest order history or the most followers is the one whose queries do the most work. Third, as the next section shows, the tail of one service becomes the ordinary case of the service that calls it.

Common mistake. Averaging percentiles. If server A has a p99 of 200 ms and server B has a p99 of 800 ms, the p99 of the pair is not 500 ms. It depends on how many requests each served and on the full shape of both distributions. To combine measurements, merge the underlying histograms and compute the percentile from the result. Monitoring systems that store histograms can do this, and those that store only precomputed percentiles cannot.

The tail at scale

Few requests are answered by one server. A page is usually assembled by a service that calls several others, each of which may call more. This is fan-out, and it has an uncomfortable effect on the tail.

Suppose each backend is fast 99% of the time, meaning its own p99 is acceptable and only 1 call in 100 is slow. A request that needs all of them is fast only if every call is fast. With independent calls, that probability is 0.99 multiplied by itself once per call:

chance that at least one of N calls is slow = 1 − 0.99N N = 1 1.0% N = 10 9.6% N = 100 63.4%

With a hundred backend calls, nearly two-thirds of user requests experience what each backend considers a one-in-a-hundred event. Jeffrey Dean and Luiz André Barroso described this effect at Google in a 2013 paper, “The Tail at Scale”, which is the standard reference on the subject.

With 20 backend calls per request, about 18.2% of user requests wait on at least one call that is slower than the backend’s own p99.

The defenses follow from the arithmetic. Each gets a fuller treatment in .

  • Call fewer things. Every dependency removed from the path removes its tail as well.
  • Set a timeout on every call. A call with no timeout can hold a request for as long as the slowest thing downstream cares to take.
  • Make parts optional. If recommendations have not answered in 150 ms, render the page without them. A complete page late is usually worse than a nearly complete page on time.
  • Hedge. Send the same read to two replicas, or send a second copy if the first has not answered by its p95, and use whichever returns first. This cuts the tail sharply for a small amount of extra load. It is safe only for operations that can be repeated without side effects.
  • Hold backends to a stricter target than the page. If a page that makes 20 calls is to be fast 99% of the time, each backend has to be fast about 99.95% of the time.

How fast is fast enough

Targets should come from what people notice. Three thresholds, published by Robert Miller in 1968 and popularized for interface design by Jakob Nielsen, have held up for decades:

DelayHow it feelsImplication
Up to about 100 msInstant. The system seems to react directly to the action.The target for typing, dragging and tapping.
Up to about 1 secondNoticeable, and the user’s train of thought is not broken.The target for navigating to a new page or view.
Up to about 10 secondsThe limit of attention. Beyond it people switch to something else.Show progress, or do the work in the background and notify.

A latency target is then written as a percentile, a threshold and a place of measurement: “99% of feed requests complete within 300 ms, measured at the load balancer over 30 days”. Each of those three parts is needed. Without the percentile it is an average, without the threshold it is a wish, and without the measuring point two teams will each believe they met it. turns such statements into formal objectives.

Does speed matter commercially?Many companies have reported that slower pages reduce sales and engagement, and the direction of the effect is not in doubt. The specific figures that circulate, such as a fixed percentage of revenue lost per 100 ms, come from individual experiments at particular companies years ago. Treat them as illustrations and measure the effect on your own product.

Throughput and the cost of being busy

Now to the second measure. A server that needs 20 ms of work per request can, in principle, complete 50 requests per second on one worker. It is tempting to conclude that it will serve 49 requests per second comfortably. It will not, and the reason is queueing.

Requests do not arrive evenly spaced. They come in clumps and gaps. When two arrive close together, the second waits for the first. On a lightly loaded server such collisions are rare and brief. As the load rises, the server is more often busy when a request arrives, the queue is more often already occupied, and each waiting request delays the ones behind it.

Utilization is the fraction of time the server is busy: the arrival rate divided by the rate it can serve. For the simplest model of a queue, with one server and randomly timed arrivals, the average response time is the service time divided by the idle fraction:

average response time = service time ÷ (1 − utilization)

The shape of this curve is one of the most important facts in system design. Going from 50% to 80% utilization gets you 60% more throughput and costs two and a half times the response time. Going from 90% to 95% gets you about 5% more throughput and doubles it again. Near the limit, a small burst of traffic produces a large jump in latency, and the tail grows faster than the average.

How exact is the formula?It is the result for the M/M/1 queue of queueing theory, which assumes one server and fully random arrival and service times. Real services have many workers and less randomness, which pushes the bend to the right: a pool of 16 workers stays responsive at higher utilization than a single worker does. The bend never disappears, and it is always sharper than intuition suggests.

Three practical rules follow.

  • Spare capacity is what keeps latency low. A fleet running at 50 to 70% of its limit at peak is correctly sized. One running at 95% is a single retry storm away from an outage.
  • Scale before the bend. Autoscaling rules that add servers at 60 or 70% CPU exist because of this curve. Waiting for 90% means adding capacity after users have already felt it.
  • Bound every queue. A queue that can grow without limit turns overload into ever-rising latency for everyone. A queue with a limit turns it into fast rejections for some, which callers can handle. This is the idea behind load shedding and backpressure.

Little’s law: how slowness becomes an outage

One more relationship ties the two measures together. In any stable system, the average number of requests in progress equals the arrival rate times the average time each one spends inside:

requests in progress = arrival rate × response time

This is Little’s law, proved by John Little in 1961, and it holds whatever the distribution of arrivals or service times. Its use is in sizing anything that is held for the duration of a request: threads, memory, database connections.

A service receives 200 requests per second and answers in 50 ms. On average 200 × 0.05 = 10 requests are in progress, and a pool of 20 database connections is plenty. Now the database slows down and the response time rises to 2 seconds. The arrival rate has not changed, yet 200 × 2 = 400 requests are in progress. The connection pool, the thread pool and perhaps the server’s memory are exhausted. New requests cannot even start, health checks time out, and the load balancer removes the server, which pushes its share of traffic onto its neighbors.

That sequence is how most outages unfold. Nothing crashed. Something became slow, and slowness multiplied the amount of work in progress until a limit was reached. It is the reason this book treats a slow dependency as more dangerous than a dead one: a dead dependency fails fast, and a slow one holds your resources while it fails.

Trading one measure for the other

Latency and throughput can often be exchanged, and the main currency is batching. Doing work in groups spreads a fixed cost over many items, so more gets done per second, and each item waits for its group to fill.

WhereThe batchGain in throughputCost in latency
Database commitsFlush many transactions to disk in one writeFar more commits per second from the same diskEach commit waits a few milliseconds for the group
Message producersCollect messages for a few milliseconds before sendingFewer, larger network requests and better compressionThe collection delay, on every message
Network sendsHold small writes until a full packet is readyFewer tiny packetsSmall interactive messages are delayed
Bulk APIsOne request carrying 500 itemsOne round trip instead of 500The first item is not processed until the whole request arrives

The choice depends on what the work is for. Analytics events can sit in a batch for five seconds and nobody will know. A keystroke in a collaborative editor cannot wait fifty milliseconds. Many systems split their traffic along this line and run an online path tuned for latency beside an offline or batch path tuned for throughput.

Where latency comes from, and how to remove it

Almost every performance technique in the rest of this book is one of six moves. When a system is slow, find the largest segment of the response time and ask which of these applies to it.

MoveExamplesCovered in
Do not do the workCache the result, return 304 Not Modified, precompute the answer

Do it closerCDN, edge servers, regional replicas
Do it at the same timeCall independent backends in parallel instead of one after another

Parallelism deserves one caution. Calling five backends at once makes the response time the slowest of the five instead of their sum, which is a large gain. It does not reduce the total work, and it widens the fan-out, so the tail arithmetic from earlier in the chapter applies in full.

Measuring it properly

You cannot improve what you measure badly, and latency is easy to measure badly.

  • Record a distribution. Store histograms or raw timings and report p50, p95 and p99. An average on a dashboard hides exactly the events you most need to see.
  • Measure where the user is. The server’s own timer starts after the request has crossed the network and waited in any queue in front of the application. A server can report 20 ms while the user waits two seconds. Measure at the client or the edge as well, and compare.
  • Measure errors beside latency. A service that fails instantly looks wonderfully fast. Track latency for successful and failed requests separately.
  • Break it down. A single number tells you there is a problem. A trace that shows the time spent in each downstream call tells you where it is. This is the purpose of distributed tracing, covered in .
  • Test at realistic load. Latency at 5% utilization predicts nothing about latency at 70%. Use the curve above to decide what load a test must reach.
Coordinated omissionMany load-testing tools send a request, wait for the answer, then send the next. If the server stalls for five seconds, the tool sends nothing during the stall and records one slow request, where real users would have sent hundreds that all waited. The result badly understates the tail. Gil Tene named this problem and it is worth knowing before trusting any benchmark. Tools that send at a fixed rate regardless of responses avoid it.

In an interview. When asked how you would make something faster, resist listing technologies. Say which part of the response time you think dominates and why, then pick the move that addresses it: “this is a read of data that rarely changes, so the service time is mostly a database query we could avoid with a cache; the trade-off is staleness, which is acceptable for profile data for a minute or so.” Mention the percentile you are targeting. Saying “p99” where others say “average” is a small signal that you have operated a real system.

  • Scalability. Adding servers moves each one back down the curve. is about making that possible.
  • Reliability. Timeouts, bounded queues and load shedding are latency tools and availability tools at once. See .

  • User experience. Perceived speed can be improved without changing real latency, through skeleton screens, optimistic updates and progress indicators. That is interface design, and it complements everything here.

Key points

  • Latency is the time for one request. Throughput is requests completed per second. Bandwidth is only the ceiling on throughput.
  • Response time is network time plus waiting plus service time. Under load, waiting is the part that grows.
  • Report percentiles. The mean hides the tail, and the tail is what users remember.
  • With fan-out, a rare slow call in each backend becomes a common slow request for the caller: with 100 calls, a 1% event affects 63% of requests.
  • Response time rises sharply as utilization approaches 100%. Keep headroom, scale before the bend and bound every queue.
  • Requests in progress equal arrival rate times response time. A slow dependency fills every pool that is held per request.
  • Batching buys throughput with latency. Choose per workload.

Check yourself

A dashboard shows an average response time of 80 ms, and users complain the app is slow. What do you check first?

The percentiles, and where the measurement is taken. A mean of 80 ms is compatible with a p99 of several seconds. And if the 80 ms is measured inside the application server, it leaves out network time and any queueing in front of the application.

A page makes 30 backend calls in parallel. Each backend has a p99 of 100 ms. Roughly what fraction of page loads will include at least one call slower than 100 ms?

1 − 0.9930, which is about 26%. Roughly one page load in four, even though each backend is slow only one time in a hundred.

Your servers run at 85% CPU at peak, and finance asks why you want more of them when 15% is unused. What is the answer?

That response time is roughly proportional to 1 ÷ (1 − utilization). At 85% the simple model already gives almost seven times the service time, and a small traffic spike or the loss of one server pushes the rest toward 95% and beyond, where latency multiplies again. The unused 15% is what absorbs bursts and failures.

A service handles 500 requests per second with a 40 ms response time. How many requests are in progress on average? What happens to that number if a dependency slows the response time to 1 second?

By Little’s law, 500 × 0.04 = 20 in progress. At 1 second it becomes 500 in progress, with no change in traffic. Any pool sized for the normal case, such as 50 database connections, is exhausted.

When is it wrong to hedge a request by sending it twice?

When repeating it has an effect: charging a card, sending an email, incrementing a counter. Hedging is safe for reads and for writes that are designed to be repeatable. covers how to make them so.
