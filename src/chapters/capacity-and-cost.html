Enough, and not much more

produced rough numbers from assumptions: requests per second, bytes stored, bits per second. They were good enough to choose an architecture. They are not good enough to decide how many machines to rent, because they rest on guesses such as “one server handles 1,000 requests a second”. Capacity planning replaces the guesses with measurements.

It has four parts, and the chapter follows them.

  1. Measure what one unit of the system can do, by testing it.
  2. Decide how much spare capacity to hold, and for what.
  3. Follow demand as it changes, by forecast and by autoscaling.
  4. Price the result, and change the design where the price is wrong.

The last part is not an afterthought. Cost is a design constraint like latency or availability. A design that meets every technical goal and costs more than the product earns has failed.

Measuring capacity: the load test

A load test sends a system controlled, artificial traffic and measures how it responds. The aim is a curve: latency and throughput against load.

To make this concrete, a small PostgreSQL 16 database was tested for this chapter with pgbench, its standard benchmarking tool, on a machine with two CPU cores. Each transaction updates three rows, reads one and inserts one. Arrivals were scheduled at a fixed rate, whether or not earlier ones had finished, and each rate ran for twelve seconds.

Usable capacity is not maximum throughput

The largest number in the table’s second column is about 4,500, and it is tempting to call that the capacity. It is not a number you can plan on. At that rate the median transaction takes over a hundred milliseconds and nothing is left for a burst.

The useful definition includes the objective: usable capacity is the highest load at which the system still meets its latency target. This function reads it from test results.

type Row = { offered: number; completed: number; p50: number; p99: number };   // rates per second, latency in ms

/** The highest offered rate at which the system kept up and stayed inside the latency objective. */
export function usableCapacity(rows: Row[], p99LimitMs: number): number {
  let best = 0;
  for (const r of [...rows].sort((a, b) => a.offered - b.offered)) {
    const keptUp = r.completed >= 0.98 * r.offered;
    if (!keptUp || r.p99 > p99LimitMs) break;       // stop at the first failure: later passes are luck
    best = r.offered;
  }
  return best;
}

With an objective of 99% of transactions under 50 ms, the measured data gives 3,500 a second: 78% of the maximum. With an objective of 10 ms it gives 1,000, under a quarter. The same machine has different capacities for different promises, and a capacity quoted without a latency target means little.

Open and closed load

The test above scheduled arrivals at a fixed rate. That is an open model, and it matches the internet: users do not wait for each other. Many load tools default to a closed model instead, in which a fixed number of simulated clients each send a request, wait for the answer, and only then send the next.

The same database under a closed test gave these results.

Two lessons are in this table. First, past sixteen clients more concurrency made the database do less work, because the transactions spent their time contending with each other. This is why connection pools are kept small, as advised.

Second, and more dangerous: the closed test never shows a latency worse than 17 ms, while the open test of the same system reached two seconds. A closed test slows itself down when the system slows down. Each client politely waits, so the queue that real users would form never appears in the results. A closed test is fine for finding maximum throughput. For latency under load, test with an open model, and measure each request from the moment it was supposed to start.

A test is only as good as its resemblance to production. Use realistic data volumes, since a query that is fast on a thousand rows may be slow on a billion. Use a realistic mix of requests, including the expensive ones. Include the dependencies, or know which you have replaced with stand-ins. The stress test deserves particular attention: knowing how the system fails, slowly or all at once, and whether it recovers when the load drops, matters as much as knowing where. continues that thread.

Headroom

Usable capacity for one instance is the starting point. A fleet needs more than the peak divided by that number, and it helps to name what the extra is for.

  • The daily peak. Traffic follows the day. For consumer products the busiest hour is commonly two to four times the average, and a planned event can be far more.
  • Room to stay fast. The gap between usable capacity and the maximum, as measured above.
  • Failure. The fleet must carry the peak with a node, or a whole zone, missing. With three zones that means each zone running at no more than two thirds, the arithmetic from .
  • Growth and lead time. Capacity must cover the growth expected before more can be added. In a cloud that may be minutes. For hardware you buy, it is months.

The fourth result is typical and often a surprise. A fleet sized responsibly for its peak spends most of its life mostly idle. That is the opportunity autoscaling addresses, and the reason pay-per-use services such as those in can be cheap for uneven workloads.

Knowing what is coming

Demand has three components, and each is planned for differently.

ComponentLooks likeHandled by
CyclesThe daily and weekly rhythm, and seasonsAutoscaling, or scheduled capacity changes
TrendSteady growth in users and in dataA forecast, reviewed each quarter, that triggers purchases, quota requests and re-architecture in time
EventsA launch, a sale, a mention in the newsPlanned capacity added beforehand, load tests at the expected level, and limits for what exceeds it

Two points are easy to miss. Stored data only grows, so storage planning follows the trend whatever traffic does, and a database that is comfortable today has a date on which it will not be. And cloud accounts have quotas on nearly everything. The capacity may exist at the provider and still be unavailable to you until a limit is raised, which can take days. Check quotas before the event, not during it.

Autoscaling policy

described the mechanism: measure a signal, compare it with a target, add or remove copies. The policy questions are which signal, what target, and how fast in each direction.

To see the trade-offs, here is one simulated day. Demand follows a wave between 1,600 and 4,000 requests a second, with an unplanned spike of 2,500 more at 13:00. Each instance has a hard limit of 250 a second and a target of 150. New instances take three minutes to become ready, and capacity is removed after ten quiet minutes.

type Policy = { usable: number; min: number; max: number; startDelayMin: number; scaleInAfterMin: number };
type Day = { instanceHours: number; shortMinutes: number; worstShortfall: number; peakInstances: number; series: { demand: number; ready: number }[] };

/** Steps one minute at a time. New instances take startDelayMin to become ready. */
export function simulateDay(demandAt: (minute: number) => number, p: Policy): Day {
  let ready = Math.max(p.min, Math.ceil(demandAt(0) / p.usable));
  const starting: number[] = [];                 // minutes at which pending instances become ready
  let lowSince = -1, instanceMinutes = 0, shortMinutes = 0, worst = 0, peakInstances = ready;
  const series: { demand: number; ready: number }[] = [];
  for (let m = 0; m < 1440; m++) {
    while (starting.length && starting[0] <= m) { starting.shift(); ready++; }
    const demand = demandAt(m);
    const wanted = Math.min(p.max, Math.max(p.min, Math.ceil(demand / p.usable)));
    const have = ready + starting.length;
    if (wanted > have) { for (let i = have; i < wanted; i++) starting.push(m + p.startDelayMin); lowSince = -1; }
    else if (wanted < ready) {
      if (lowSince < 0) lowSince = m;
      if (m - lowSince >= p.scaleInAfterMin) { ready = Math.max(wanted, p.min); lowSince = -1; }
    } else lowSince = -1;
    const capacity = ready * p.usable;
    if (demand > capacity) { shortMinutes++; worst = Math.max(worst, demand - capacity); }
    instanceMinutes += ready + starting.length;  // an instance is paid for while it starts
    peakInstances = Math.max(peakInstances, ready + starting.length);
    series.push({ demand, ready });
  }
  return { instanceHours: instanceMinutes / 60, shortMinutes, worstShortfall: worst, peakInstances, series };
}

The simulation gave three results worth stating as numbers.

QuestionResult
Saving on the daily wave, against 27 instances always on471 instance-hours instead of 648: 27% fewer
Minutes over the hard limit during the spikeEqual to the start-up delay: 1 minute at 1, 3 at 3, 10 at 10. Load reached 118% of the limit
The same spike with a target of 125 for each instance instead of 150No minutes over the limit, for 562 instance-hours: 19% more

They summarize what autoscaling can and cannot do.

It follows what is slower than its own reaction time. A daily wave changes over hours and is tracked easily. A spike that arrives in seconds is over the limit for exactly as long as new capacity takes to start. Making instances start faster, with smaller images, less work at start-up or instances kept warm in reserve, shortens every such gap.

The target sets what a spike can be absorbed without scaling. At a target of 60% of the limit, the fleet can take a sudden rise of two thirds before it is overloaded. Lowering the target buys room for larger spikes at a steady cost. It is the same headroom decision as before, now expressed as one number.

Scale out quickly and in slowly. Being short of capacity costs users. Having too much for ten minutes costs a little money. The policy should be asymmetric, and it should also be damped, so that a noisy signal does not add and remove instances every minute.

Three further tools cover what reactive scaling cannot.

  • Scheduled scaling. If traffic rises at 08:00 every weekday, add capacity at 07:45.
  • Predictive scaling. The platform forecasts from recent history and scales ahead of the forecast.
  • Limits and shedding. A maximum protects the budget and downstream systems, and what exceeds capacity is rejected early, as described. Autoscaling and load shedding work as a pair: one for the load you can catch up with, the other for the load you cannot.

Autoscaling also assumes that what the service depends on can take the extra work. A database, a third-party API or a licensed limit does not grow because the web tier did. Find the first fixed constraint in the path, because that is the system’s real capacity.

How capacity is priced

Cloud providers sell the same machine three ways.

ModelYou promiseYou getSuits
On demandNothingCapacity by the second at the list priceThe variable part of load, and anything new
CommitmentTo spend a fixed amount each hour for one or three yearsA large discount on that amount, used or notThe steady base that runs all the time
Spare capacity, or spotTo give the machine back on short noticeThe deepest discount, with no guaranteeWork that can be interrupted and retried: batch jobs, build agents, stateless extras

As of October 2026, AWS advertises savings of up to 66% and 72% for two of its four kinds of commitment plan, compared with on-demand prices, for terms of one or three years. The largest figures need three years and payment in advance. Discounts for a one-year term with nothing paid up front are much smaller. Spot prices move with supply and are often a fraction of the on-demand price.

How much to commit can be computed. A commitment is paid for every hour, so it saves money in the hours when it is used and wastes money when it is not.

/** Monthly cost when `committed` instances are paid for around the clock at a discount and the rest on demand. */
export function blendedCost(hourly: number[], price: number, committed: number, discount: number): number {
  let cost = 0;
  for (const n of hourly) cost += committed * price * (1 - discount) + Math.max(0, n - committed) * price;
  return cost;
}

The general rule: commit to the load you are confident will still be there at the end of the term, and no further, because a commitment cannot follow you when an architecture changes.

Data transfer

Data transfer is the line most often left out of a design, because moving bytes inside one data center is free and feels free everywhere. In a cloud it is priced by direction and distance. These are typical AWS figures as of 2026, and other providers follow the same pattern.

Three design consequences follow. Chatty services spread across zones pay for every call: 50 TB a month between two zones, charged both ways, is $1,000. Replicating a busy database or event stream to another region is billed by the gigabyte, as noted. And because leaving costs so much more than arriving, data tends to stay with the provider it was first given to, which is worth remembering when choosing one.

Where savings usually are

In rough order of how often they pay off:

  1. Turn off what is not used. Forgotten test environments, unattached disks, old snapshots, idle load balancers. It is dull work and reliably the cheapest saving.
  2. Right-size. Instances chosen by guesswork are often much larger than their measured use requires. The same goes for the resource requests in .
  3. Autoscale and schedule. Development environments need not run at night. Production need not run its peak fleet at 4 a.m.
  4. Commit to the base. As computed above.
  5. Tier and expire data. Cheaper classes for cold data, and deletion for data with no remaining purpose, which includes most logs after some weeks, as argued.
  6. Change the design. Caching, batching, compression, a cheaper processor architecture, fewer cross-zone calls. These are the largest savings and the most work.

A bigger machine, or more machines?

introduced the choice between scaling up and scaling out and noted that it is a question of cost. Here is the comparison worked through, with assumed figures.

Within a family of cloud instances, price is roughly proportional to size, so the larger machine carries no premium. The whole difference is the engineering, and at these figures the sharding project costs as much as sixteen years of the larger instance’s extra rent. Scaling up is the cheaper choice by a wide margin.

That holds until one of three things is true. The largest available machine is not enough: clouds rent machines with hundreds of cores and terabytes of memory, which is a high ceiling and still a ceiling. Or the requirement is availability and not capacity, since one large machine is one point of failure. Or growth is fast enough that the ceiling will arrive before a sharding project could finish, in which case the project must start while the single machine still has room. The practical policy is to scale up while you can and to know the date on which you no longer can.

Cost for each thing you sell

A total bill says little by itself. Whether $300,000 a month is high depends on what it buys. Dividing by a unit of business gives a number that can be judged and tracked: cost per thousand requests, per active user, per tenant, per order.

For the photo app at list prices the bill is about $318,000 a month for 10 million daily users: a little over three cents per daily user per month. If the product earns less than that from each user, no optimization of instance types will save it, and the conversation has to be about images, delivery and revenue.

Unit costs are useful in three ways.

  • They reveal trends that totals hide. A bill that grows 20% while users grow 30% is getting better. One that grows 20% with flat users is a leak.
  • They expose expensive customers. In a multi-tenant product, cost per tenant against revenue per tenant shows who is unprofitable, using the tenant tags from .
  • They make design choices comparable. “This feature adds $0.002 to each request” can be weighed. “This feature needs more servers” cannot.

None of this works unless costs can be attributed. Tag every resource with its service, team and environment from the start, give each team a view of what it spends, set budgets with alerts, and put cost next to latency and errors on the dashboards. Teams that can see what their design costs make cheaper designs.

In an interview. After estimating load, say what you would measure before trusting the estimate: usable capacity for each instance at the latency target, from an open-model load test. Size the fleet with explicit headroom for the peak and for losing a zone. Name the autoscaling signal, target and limits. If the system moves many bytes, say that delivery and storage will dominate the cost and what you would do about it. One sentence on cost per user or per request shows you are designing a business, not only a system.

Common mistakes

  • Planning from a benchmark. Somebody else’s number, for somebody else’s workload.
  • Quoting maximum throughput as capacity. The system can do it, at a latency nobody would accept.
  • Closed-model load tests for latency. They hide the queue that real users would be in.
  • Testing with toy data. Everything is fast on a thousand rows.
  • No headroom for failure. The peak fits exactly, until a zone is lost at the peak.
  • Trusting autoscaling for spikes. It is late by its start-up time, every time.
  • Autoscaling the front and forgetting the back. The database is the real limit.
  • Committing to the peak. Paying around the clock for capacity used four hours a day.
  • Ignoring data transfer. Cross-zone chatter and internet egress discovered on the invoice.
  • Optimizing the small line. A week spent on instance types when delivery is 85% of the bill.
  • Distributing too early. A sharding project where a larger machine would have served for years.
  • No attribution. Nobody knows which service or team the money goes to.

Key points

  • Measure capacity with a load test. Usable capacity is the highest load that still meets the latency objective, well below maximum throughput.
  • Test latency with an open model, where arrivals do not wait for earlier requests. A closed model hides queueing.
  • Hold headroom for named reasons: the daily peak, staying fast, losing a zone, and growth until more can be added.
  • Autoscaling follows changes slower than its reaction time. For anything faster, the target utilization is the only cushion, with load shedding behind it.
  • Commit to the steady base at a discount, run the variable part on demand, and use interruptible capacity for work that can be retried.
  • Write the bill as quantities times prices and sort it. For systems that move data, storage and transfer dominate and compute is small.
  • Scaling up is usually far cheaper than distributing, until a ceiling, an availability need or fast growth forces the change.
  • Track cost per unit of business, and make every resource attributable to an owner.

Check yourself

In the measured load test, the database completed about 4,500 transactions a second at most. Why is 4,500 the wrong number to plan with, and what is the right one for an objective of 99% under 50 ms?

At 4,500 a second the median latency is over 100 ms and any extra load queues without limit, so the objective is badly missed. The right number is the highest rate that still met the objective, 3,500 a second, about 78% of the maximum.

A closed-model test with 64 clients reported an average latency of 17 ms. An open-model test of the same system at 5,000 arrivals a second reported a median of 654 ms. Why the difference?

In the closed test each client waits for its response before sending again, so when the system slows down the load slows with it and no queue forms. In the open test arrivals keep coming at the set rate, as real users do, so the excess queues and waiting time is counted. The open test shows what users would experience.

Peak load is 9,000 requests a second. One instance has a usable capacity of 300. The service runs in three zones and must survive the loss of one at peak. How many instances?

9,000 ÷ 300 = 30 instances must remain after a zone is lost. With three equal zones, two zones must hold 30, so each zone has 15 and the fleet is 45. In normal operation each instance then runs at two thirds of its usable capacity.

An autoscaler takes four minutes to add ready capacity. What does that imply about a traffic spike that doubles load within ten seconds?

For about four minutes the fleet has only the capacity it had before the spike. Whether that is an outage depends on the headroom: if instances were at 50% of their limit, doubling just fits. If they were at 70%, the fleet is overloaded until new capacity arrives, and load shedding has to protect it meanwhile.

A service runs between 20 and 50 instances through the day. A commitment gives a 30% discount. Why is committing to 50 instances a mistake, and roughly where is the best commitment?

A commitment is paid every hour whether used or not. Committing to 50 pays for 50 around the clock while the average in use is far lower, which can cost more than no commitment. The best level is a little above the daily minimum, where the discount on hours used still outweighs the waste in the few hours below it.

A video product stores 400 TB and sends 2 PB a month to viewers. Using $0.023 per GB-month and $0.09 per GB, which line is larger and by how much?

Storage: 400,000 GB × $0.023 = $9,200 a month. Delivery: 2,000,000 GB × $0.09 = $180,000 a month, nearly twenty times larger. Effort belongs on delivery: a CDN, better compression, lower bitrates where acceptable.

Two services in different availability zones exchange 20 TB a month in each direction. What does that cost at $0.01 per GB each way, and how could it be avoided?

20,000 GB × $0.01 × 2 directions = $400 a month, for traffic that would be free within one zone. Routing calls to an instance in the same zone where one is healthy, batching, or compressing the payloads reduces it.

A database on an 8-core instance is nearly full. Give two conditions under which sharding now is the right choice despite a 16-core instance being cheaper to adopt.

If growth is fast enough that even the largest available instance would be outgrown before a sharding project could be completed, the project has to begin now. Or if the driver is availability or data placement and not capacity, for example the need to survive the loss of the machine without a failover pause, or to keep tenants’ data in different regions.
