Why keep copies

Replication means keeping the same data on more than one machine. Each copy is a replica. There are three reasons to do it, and a system usually wants more than one of them.

  • Availability. If one machine fails, another has the data and can take over. This is the redundancy from , applied to the hardest component to make redundant.
  • Read capacity. Queries can be spread across the copies. Most applications read far more than they write, so this goes a long way.
  • Distance. A copy near the users answers them faster than one on another continent.

If data never changed, replication would be a file copy. All the difficulty is in handling changes. A write arrives at one machine and must reach the others across a network that delays and drops messages, while any of the machines may fail at any moment. Three designs exist, distinguished by where writes are accepted: at one node, at several, or at any.

Leader and followers

The most common design by far has one replica, the leader (also called the primary), accept all writes. The others, the followers (replicas, standbys or secondaries), receive a stream of the leader’s changes and apply them in the same order. Reads can be served by any of them.

What travels to the followers is the write-ahead log from , or a translation of it. Since the log is a complete, ordered record of every change, a follower that applies it ends up with the same data as the leader. There are two forms.

  • Physical replication ships the log as it is, describing changes to bytes in pages. It is simple and exact, and the follower must run the same database version, since it is a byte-level copy.
  • Logical replication ships changes as rows: “in table photos, the row with id 871 now has like_count 101”. It works across versions, can copy a subset of tables, and can feed systems that are not databases at all, which is the basis of .

The appeal of a single leader is that it avoids the hardest problem in replication. With one place where writes happen, there is one order of events and no possibility of two copies accepting conflicting changes. The price is that the leader is special: all writes depend on it, and when it fails something must replace it.

When is a write “committed”?

The most consequential setting in a replicated database is whether the leader waits for followers before telling the client that a write succeeded.

AsynchronousSynchronous
Write latencyLocal disk onlyLocal disk plus a round trip to a follower
If the leader is lostThe most recent writes, acknowledged to clients, may be goneEvery acknowledged write exists on another machine
If a follower is slow or downNo effect on writesWrites wait, or stop
Typical useRead replicas, distant regions, most default setupsData that must not be lost: payments, orders

Fully synchronous replication to every follower would mean that any single follower’s failure halts all writes, so nobody runs that. The practical arrangement, sometimes called semi-synchronous, waits for one follower out of several, or for a majority. One slow follower then does no harm, and every acknowledged write still exists in two places.

In the vocabulary of , this setting is your recovery point objective. Asynchronous replication has a small, non-zero window of data loss on failover. Synchronous replication has none and pays for it on every write. The round trip costs under a millisecond to a follower in the same building, one or two milliseconds across availability zones in a region, and tens of milliseconds between regions, which is why synchronous replication across continents is rare.

Replication lag, and what users see

An asynchronous follower is always a little behind the leader. The delay is replication lag. Normally it is milliseconds. Under heavy writes, during a large batch job, or over a slow link, it can grow to seconds or minutes. A system that reads from followers is therefore eventually consistent: if writes stopped, all copies would become identical, and at any given moment they may differ.

Users notice lag in three specific ways, and each has a named guarantee that prevents it.

What the user experiencesGuarantee that prevents itHow to provide it
“I saved it and it is gone.”Read-your-writes: a user always sees their own changesFor a short time after a user writes, send that user’s reads to the leader. Or record the log position of their write and read only from followers that have reached it.
“The comment was there, and after a refresh it vanished.”Monotonic reads: a user never sees time go backwardSend each user’s reads to the same follower, for example chosen by a hash of the user ID
“I can see the reply and not the question it answers.”Consistent prefix: changes are seen in the order they happenedKeep related data on the same replica set, so one log orders it

The simplest policy that works for most products is to read from the leader anything where staleness would be visible or harmful to the person asking, such as their own profile, their cart or their just-posted comment, and to read from followers everything shared and tolerant, such as other people’s feeds, search results and counts. places these guarantees in the wider picture.

Common mistake. Adding read replicas and routing all reads to them without changing any code. It works in testing, where lag is zero. In production, any code that writes and then immediately reads back, such as “create the order, then load it to render the confirmation page”, fails intermittently in a way that depends on load.

Failover

When a follower fails, little happens. It restarts, asks the leader for everything since the last position it applied, and catches up. When the leader fails, a follower must be promoted to take its place. This is failover, and it has three steps, each with a way to go wrong.

  1. Decide that the leader is dead. The only evidence available is that it has stopped responding, and a leader that is slow, overloaded or cut off by a network fault looks the same as one that has crashed. A short timeout causes failovers when nothing was wrong. A long one extends every real outage.
  2. Choose a new leader. It should be the follower with the most complete log. With asynchronous replication even that follower may lack the old leader’s last few writes, and those are lost, although clients were told they had succeeded.
  3. Redirect everyone. Clients and the other followers must start using the new leader, and, most importantly, the old leader must be prevented from continuing to act as one.

The situation in the figure is called split brain, and it is the worst outcome of a failover, because data diverges and has to be reconciled by hand. Preventing it requires that at most one node can believe it is the leader. Two mechanisms do this. Requiring a majority of nodes to agree on who leads means the side of a partition with fewer nodes cannot elect anyone. And fencing actively stops the old leader, by cutting off its access to storage or by having other systems reject requests that carry an out-of-date leadership number. covers how agreement is reached.

A case study: GitHub, October 2018

On 21 October 2018, routine maintenance on network equipment disconnected GitHub’s main data center on the US East Coast from the rest of its network for 43 seconds. The software managing its MySQL clusters, seeing the East Coast leaders unreachable, promoted followers on the West Coast. That was the system working as designed.

When the link returned, both sides held writes the other lacked. The East Coast databases had a few seconds of writes that had never been replicated, and the West Coast had been accepting new writes since the failover. Neither could simply be discarded. To protect the data, GitHub kept the West Coast as leader and spent the next day restoring the East Coast replicas from backups and letting them catch up. The site was degraded for 24 hours and 11 minutes, with out-of-date information shown and background work paused. For one of the busiest clusters, 954 writes from the affected seconds had to be reconciled afterward.

The lessons GitHub drew are worth remembering. A 43-second network fault produced a day-long incident. Automatic failover across regions made the situation worse than waiting would have, since the application was not designed for the latency of a leader on the other coast. And asynchronous replication means an acknowledged write may exist in only one place.

Multiple leaders

In multi-leader replication, more than one node accepts writes, and each sends its changes to the others. The reasons to want this are specific:

  • Writes in several regions. Users in Europe and Asia each write to a nearby leader instead of crossing the world.
  • Clients that work offline. A calendar or notes app on a phone accepts changes with no connection and synchronizes later. Each device is in effect a leader.
  • Real-time collaboration, where several people edit the same document at once.

The cost is that the problem a single leader avoids comes back: two leaders can accept changes to the same data at the same time. This is a write conflict, and it is discovered only later, when the changes meet. Something must decide the outcome.

StrategyHow it worksWeakness
Avoid conflictsRoute all writes for a given record to the same leader, for example each user’s data to their home regionBreaks down when a user moves or a region fails over
Last write winsAttach a timestamp to each write and keep the latestThe other write is silently discarded. Clocks on different machines disagree, so “latest” is unreliable.
Merge automaticallyUse data types designed so that concurrent changes combine, known as CRDTsWorks for counters, sets and text, and not for arbitrary business rules
Keep both and askStore the conflicting versions and let the application or the user resolve themComplexity moves into every reader

Multi-leader replication also weakens constraints. Two leaders can each accept a different user registering the same username, since neither sees the other’s write in time. The advice from experienced operators is consistent: avoid it unless one of the three situations above forces it, and then prefer conflict avoidance. and return to these designs.

No leader: quorums

The third design has no leader at all. Any replica accepts reads and writes, and the client, or a coordinator acting for it, talks to several replicas for each operation. It was made well known by Amazon’s Dynamo paper and is used by Cassandra, ScyllaDB and Riak.

Three numbers define the behavior. Each piece of data is stored on N replicas. A write is considered successful when W of them confirm it. A read asks R of them and takes the newest value among the answers. If W + R is greater than N, the set of replicas that confirmed any write and the set asked by any later read must share at least one member, so the read is certain to see that write.

Every read reaches at least one replica that holds the latest acknowledged write, because W + R = 4 is greater than N = 3. Writes continue with 1 of 3 replicas down, and reads with 1 down.

The common configuration is N = 3 with W = 2 and R = 2, which tolerates one failed replica for both reads and writes. The numbers are a dial. W = 1 gives the fastest writes and loses the guarantee. R = 1 with W = N gives fast reads and writes that fail if any replica is down. Cassandra lets each query choose its own level.

Bringing replicas back in line

Since a write may miss some replicas, the system needs ways to bring them up to date. Dynamo-style stores use three, each covering what the one before it misses.

  • Hinted handoff. When a replica is unreachable, its write is given to another node, which stores it with a note saying whose it is, the hint, and delivers it when the owner returns. If such stand-in nodes are allowed to count toward W, the arrangement is called a sloppy quorum. It keeps writes available during an outage, and it weakens the overlap guarantee, because a read that asks the proper replicas may not reach the stand-in.
  • Read repair. When a read receives different versions from the replicas it asked, the coordinator returns the newest and writes it back to the stale ones. This heals data that is read, and never touches data that is not.
  • Anti-entropy. A background process compares whole replicas and copies whatever differs. It catches rarely read data and hints that were lost.

Comparing two replicas key by key would mean sending all the data across the network to find out that nearly all of it matches. Replicas compare summaries instead, arranged as a Merkle tree. Each leaf is the hash of one range of keys. Each node above is the hash of its two children, so the single hash at the root summarizes everything beneath it.

Two replicas exchange root hashes. If they are equal, the replicas are identical and the comparison is over. If not, they compare the children and descend only into the branches that differ. The work is in proportion to how much the replicas differ, not to how much data they hold. A tree over a million key ranges is about twenty levels deep, so a single differing range is found by comparing a few dozen hashes. Cassandra builds these trees when a repair is run, and the same structure verifies data in Git, in ZFS and in blockchains.

Leaderless systems have no failover, which is their main attraction: a node failing requires no election and no promotion, and the others simply carry on. The costs are subtler than they first appear. Two clients writing the same key at the same time produce a conflict, exactly as in the multi-leader case, and the usual resolution is last write wins. Even with overlapping quorums, edge cases around failed writes and concurrent operations mean the guarantee is weaker than a single leader’s. These systems suit data where each key is written independently and occasional overwrites are acceptable, and they are a poor fit for anything that needs transactions.

Replication built on consensus

Most distributed databases designed in the last decade take a fourth route that combines the best of the above. Each piece of data has a small group of replicas, typically three or five, that run a consensus protocol such as Raft. There is a leader, so writes have one order. A write is committed when a majority of the group has it, so no acknowledged write is lost when a minority fails. And if the leader fails, the group elects a new one by itself, with the majority rule making split brain impossible.

This is what CockroachDB, YugabyteDB, TiDB, Google Spanner and etcd do, and MongoDB’s replica sets work in a closely related way. The price is that every write waits for a majority, and that at least three nodes are needed to tolerate one failure. explains the protocol.

Three practical points apply whichever you use.

  • Replicas scale reads and not writes. Every follower must apply every write the leader does. If the leader is saturated by writes, adding followers does nothing for it. The answer to write load is .
  • Monitor lag and alert on it. A follower that is minutes behind is serving wrong answers, and it is a poor candidate for failover.
  • Practice failover. A failover that has never been exercised will not work as documented the first time it is needed. Check what your application does in the seconds when the leader changes: connections break, and transactions in flight fail and need retrying.

In production. Replication is not a backup. A mistaken DELETE, a bad migration or an application bug that corrupts rows is replicated to every copy within seconds, faithfully. Recovery from mistakes needs backups that can be restored to a point in time before the mistake ().

In an interview. Say “a leader with two replicas, one synchronous in another zone for failover and one asynchronous for reads”, and then address the consequence: “reads from the replica may be slightly stale, so a user’s own data is read from the leader”. Mentioning lag and read-your-writes without being asked distinguishes a design that has met production from one that has not.

Common mistakes

  • Reading from a replica straight after writing and assuming the write is there.
  • Treating replicas as a way to scale writes.
  • Assuming asynchronous replication loses nothing on failover.
  • Automatic failover with aggressive timeouts and no protection against two leaders.
  • Never testing failover, including how the application reconnects.
  • No monitoring of replication lag.
  • Using multi-leader replication for convenience and discovering conflicts in production.
  • Relying on timestamps from different machines to order writes.
  • Calling replication a backup.

Key points

  • Replication provides availability, read capacity and proximity. The difficulty is keeping copies consistent while data changes.
  • A single leader gives writes one order and avoids conflicts. It is the default, and it requires failover.
  • Asynchronous replication is fast and can lose recent acknowledged writes. Synchronous replication loses none and adds latency to each write.
  • Followers lag. Decide which reads may be stale, and send the rest to the leader.
  • Failover is the dangerous moment: writes can be lost, and two nodes can both believe they lead unless a majority rule or fencing prevents it.
  • Multi-leader and leaderless designs accept writes in several places and must resolve conflicts afterward.
  • With N replicas, a write quorum W and a read quorum R overlap when W + R is greater than N.
  • Replicas scale reads, not writes, and replication is not a backup.

Check yourself

A user updates their display name and the next page still shows the old one. Refreshing a few seconds later shows the new one. What is happening and how would you fix it?

The write went to the leader and the following read was served by a follower that had not yet received it. This is a read-your-writes violation caused by replication lag. Send the user’s reads of their own data to the leader for a short time after they write, or track the log position of the write and read only from a follower that has reached it.

With asynchronous replication, the leader fails and a follower is promoted. A customer says their order was confirmed and now does not exist. Is that possible?

Yes. The leader acknowledged the order before sending it to any follower, and failed before it was replicated. The promoted follower never had it. Preventing this needs synchronous replication to at least one follower for writes that must not be lost.

Why can a leader that is merely slow cause more damage than one that has crashed?

A crashed leader stops accepting writes. A slow or disconnected one may be declared dead and replaced while it is still running, and it can keep accepting writes from clients that still reach it. Two leaders then diverge, which is split brain. Fencing or a majority requirement is needed to stop the old one.

A cluster stores each item on 5 replicas. Writes wait for 3 confirmations and reads ask 2 replicas. Can a read miss the latest write?

Yes. W + R is 5, which is not greater than N = 5, so the two replicas asked by the read can be exactly the two that the write did not reach. Asking 3 replicas would guarantee an overlap.

Your leader’s CPU is saturated, and analysis shows most of the load is writes. Will adding three read replicas help?

Not much. Moving reads elsewhere frees a little capacity, and the write load stays entirely on the leader. Each replica also has to apply the same writes. Write load is reduced by doing fewer or cheaper writes, by a larger machine, or by partitioning the data across several leaders.

Two regions each have a leader, and a user changes their email address in both at nearly the same moment. What decides the result under last write wins, and why is that fragile?

The write with the later timestamp is kept and the other is discarded without notice. The timestamps come from two different machines whose clocks are not perfectly synchronized, so “later” may not reflect the real order, and one user’s change vanishes either way.
