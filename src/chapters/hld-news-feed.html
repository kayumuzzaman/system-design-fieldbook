The problem

The problem. Design the home feed of a social network. Users follow other users and publish short posts, with images. When a user opens the app, they see the posts of the accounts they follow, the most interesting first, and can scroll back through older ones.

A news feed is the list of recent posts from the accounts a user follows, assembled for that user each time they look. The difficulty is that every feed is different and every post belongs to many feeds. One post must reach thousands or millions of readers, and one feed draws on hundreds of authors. Somewhere, the system pays for every follow, either when a post is written or when a feed is read. The design is the choice of where, and the few accounts with enormous audiences make each simple choice fail. The method of takes these in order.

The feed list holds ids, not posts. A post is stored once and its id copied; an edit or a delete then changes one record, and the feed service filters out ids whose posts are gone when it fills in a page.

Step 5: The design

Publishing and reading are separate services, scaled for different loads. Everything that depends on followers runs in the dashed band, after the author has their answer: publishing a post never waits for its 200 or 100 million followers. The queue between them absorbs bursts, such as a sports final, when posts arrive several times faster than usual ().

Two choices in the flows are deliberate. The post service adds the post to the author’s own lists before it answers, which gives the author read-your-writes while followers wait for the workers. And the feed service reads the graph only in the form of a few big accounts’ timelines; the work of following 200 accounts was done earlier, by the workers.

Step 6a: Fan-out on write or on read

There are two ways to assemble a feed. Fan-out on write copies the id of each new post into a stored feed list for every follower when the post is published, so reading a feed is one list. Fan-out on read stores each post only in its author’s timeline and builds a feed when it is requested, by merging the timelines of every account the reader follows. Both give the same feed. The chapter’s code implements both, and a hybrid of the two, over the same follow graph; in a test on 3,000 accounts, all of them returned the same two pages for 6,000 reads after 30,000 posts.

Both rest on one operation: merging lists of ids, each already sorted newest first, into one. A heap holding the head of each list gives the next newest id in a few steps, however many lists there are.

// Merges several newest-first lists into one, taking `limit` ids. Each list is a Capped plus a position.
function merge(lists: Capped[], limit: number, before: number, work: Work): number[] {
  type Head = { list: Capped; i: number };
  const heap = new MinHeap((a, b) => a.list.at(a.i) > b.list.at(b.i));
  for (const list of lists) {
    work.timelinesRead++;
    const i = list.start(before);
    if (i >= 0) heap.push({ list, i });
  }
  const out: number[] = [];
  while (out.length < limit && heap.size > 0) {
    const h = heap.pop() as Head;
    out.push(h.list.at(h.i));
    work.entriesScanned++;
    if (h.i > 0) heap.push({ list: h.list, i: h.i - 1 });
  }
  return out;
}

To compare the costs, the simulation builds a network of 100,000 accounts with 6,002,235 follows, in which popularity is skewed as it is on real networks. The average account has 60 followers, the median 16 and the largest 20,493, and the 100 most followed accounts hold 16.6% of all follows. Fan-out on write costs 62.0 list inserts per post on average (the author’s timeline, the author’s own feed and 60 followers) and one list per feed read. Fan-out on read costs one insert per post and 61.0 lists per read, and a reader at the 99th percentile merges 282.

The two numbers add up to about 63 under every strategy, and that is no accident. Each follow is paid for once: either by a write into the follower’s list each time the author posts, or by a read of the author’s timeline each time the follower opens the feed. A strategy only chooses which side pays. That makes the choice a matter of rates. Silberstein and colleagues showed in 2010 that the cheapest plan decides for each pair of reader and author: copy ahead when the reader reads more often than the author posts, after weighting by the costs of a write and a read. Step 2 found 50 feed reads for every post, so for nearly every pair, copying ahead wins.

With 50 reads for each post, pure fan-out on write costs 112 operations for a post and its reads, and pure fan-out on read 3,052, 27 times as many. Any account exempted from fan-out moves its followers’ share to the expensive side. So the starting design is fan-out on write for everyone, and the question becomes which accounts it cannot serve.

Step 6b: The celebrity problem

The celebrity problem is the cost and delay of fanning out a post from an account with millions of followers: one post becomes millions of writes, all due at once. It is the hot spot of in a new form. At the 139,000 entries a second of step 2, a post to 100 million followers is 12 minutes of the whole fleet’s work, and the 5-second delivery target is gone for everyone queued behind it.

The answer is the hybrid in the code below. Accounts above a follower threshold are not fanned out. Their posts go only to their own timeline, and each feed read merges in the timelines of the few such accounts the reader follows.

// The hybrid: accounts with at least `threshold` followers are not fanned out.
// Their posts stay on their own timeline, and readers merge in the few such accounts they follow.
export class HybridFeed implements FeedStrategy {
  readonly name: string;
  readonly work: Work = newWork();
  private readonly graph: Graph;
  private readonly threshold: number;
  private readonly feeds: Capped[];
  private readonly timelines: Capped[];

  constructor(graph: Graph, cap: number, threshold: number) {
    this.graph = graph;
    this.threshold = threshold;
    this.name = `hybrid, threshold ${threshold}`;
    this.feeds = Array.from({ length: graph.users }, () => new Capped(cap));
    this.timelines = Array.from({ length: graph.users }, () => new Capped(cap));
  }
  isCelebrity(user: number): boolean {
    return followerCount(this.graph, user) >= this.threshold;
  }
  publish(post: Post): void {
    const g = this.graph, a = post.author;
    (this.timelines[a] as Capped).add(post.id);
    this.work.inserts++;
    if (this.isCelebrity(a)) return;                       // read-time merge instead
    this.insert(a, post.id);
    for (let k = g.followerStart[a] as number; k < (g.followerStart[a + 1] as number); k++) {
      this.insert(g.followers[k] as number, post.id);
    }
  }
  private insert(user: number, id: number): void {
    (this.feeds[user] as Capped).add(id);
    this.work.inserts++;
  }
  read(user: number, limit: number, before = Infinity): number[] {
    const g = this.graph;
    const lists = [this.feeds[user] as Capped];
    if (this.isCelebrity(user)) lists.push(this.timelines[user] as Capped);
    for (let k = g.followeeStart[user] as number; k < (g.followeeStart[user + 1] as number); k++) {
      const v = g.followees[k] as number;
      if (this.isCelebrity(v)) lists.push(this.timelines[v] as Capped);
    }
    return merge(lists, limit, before, this.work);
  }
}

One job per post leaves the big post on one worker for 400 seconds, and even an ordinary account with 20,000 followers takes 4 seconds. Batches spread a post over all workers, but with one queue the big post’s 2,000 batches stand in front of every ordinary post: those published right after it took 25 seconds to deliver, and the backlog took 40 seconds to clear. A second queue for posts with more than 100 batches, served only when the first is empty, keeps ordinary posts under half a second and lets the big post use the spare capacity. That spare capacity sets the threshold: 80,000 minus 30,000 is 50,000 entries a second, so an account with more than 250,000 followers cannot be delivered in 5 seconds even with every worker on it. At the scale of step 2, with workers sized for twice the peak of 278,000 entries a second, the same rule puts the threshold near 1.4 million followers, an estimate.

Exempting an account moves its load, it does not remove it. Its timeline is read by every feed request of its followers: if 30% of users follow it, 20,000 reads a second at peak land on one list. That list is copied to many nodes of the cache, and each feed server keeps it in memory for a second or two, as describes for a hot key.

Step 6c: Ranking

A feed in time order is simple and predictable, and the API above serves it with an id cursor. A ranked feed puts the posts the reader is most likely to care about first. It runs in stages. Candidate generation collects the posts that might be shown: here, the 800 ids of the reader’s list and the recent posts of the big accounts they follow. Each candidate then gets a score from features such as its age, how often the reader interacts with its author, and how much engagement it is drawing. Facebook has described its score as a weighted sum of predicted probabilities that the reader will like, comment on or share the post.

The full model is the expensive part, so it should not score more candidates than it must. The simulation gave 1,000 readers 800 candidates each, spread over 72 hours, and a full model whose score halves every 12 hours of age. It then let the full model score only some candidates, chosen either by age or by a cheap model whose scores correlate with the full model’s at 0.82, and counted how much of the best page of 20 was found.

Scoring the newest 100 finds 58% of the best page, because a strong post from yesterday loses to weak posts from this morning. A cheap first pass that picks 100 finds 86%, and 200 finds 97%. That is the shape of every production ranking system: a cheap stage over many candidates, an expensive one over a few hundred.

Ranking changes paging. A post’s rank changes as it ages and draws engagement, so ranking again for page 2 shows some posts twice and skips others. The test measured it: re-ranking for page 2 half an hour after page 1 repeated 4.05 of page 1’s posts on average, and 3.06 older posts that had climbed above position 20 were never shown at all. Serving page 2 from the order saved with page 1 repeated none. So the feed service saves the ranked list for the session, the cursor is a position in it, and new posts wait for the next refresh. A chronological feed has the same problem with offsets: in the test, four new posts made page 2 by offset repeat four posts, and page 2 by id cursor repeated none.

At ten times the load, feed lists need 115 TB of memory in three copies. The answer is to keep lists in memory only for active users, as Twitter described, and to build a returning user’s feed once on read. Fan-out reaches 1.4 million entries a second, more workers on more partitions of the same queue. At a hundred times, one region cannot hold it: users are split into regional cells, each with its own workers and feed cache, and posts are copied between regions so that each fans out to its local followers (). The hybrid, the merge and the ranking stages do not change.

In the real world

  • Twitter, 2012. In a QCon talk, Raffi Krikorian described home timelines kept in a Redis cluster, at most 800 entries each, in three copies, with 300,000 timeline reads a second against 6,000 writes. Fan-out aimed to deliver within 5 seconds, and a tweet could take up to 5 minutes to reach Lady Gaga’s 31 million followers. The largest accounts were no longer fanned out and were merged in when timelines were read, and only active users were kept in memory (as summarized by High Scalability, July 2013).
  • Twitter’s ranked timeline, 2023. When Twitter published its recommendation code in March 2023, it said that each request picks the best 1,500 posts from hundreds of millions, half on average from accounts the reader follows, ranks them with a neural network of about 48 million parameters that predicts ten kinds of engagement, and that the pipeline runs about 5 billion times a day in under 1.5 seconds on average.
  • Facebook, 2021. Meta’s engineers described more than 1,000 posts available to each user each day on average, a lightweight pass that selects about 500, a main pass that scores each with a weighted sum of predicted engagement, and a final pass that adjusts the order for context.
  • The per-pair rule. Silberstein, Terrace, Cooper and Ramakrishnan (SIGMOD 2010) proved that the cheapest plan chooses copy-ahead or gather-on-read separately for each pair of reader and author, from the ratio of the reader’s request rate to the author’s posting rate.

Key points

  • Every follow is paid for once: by a write when the author posts, or by a read when the follower opens the feed.
  • With 50 feed reads per post, copying ids ahead into each follower’s list is the cheaper default, 27 times cheaper in the simulation.
  • Accounts too large to fan out within the delivery target are merged in at read time. Set the threshold from the workers’ spare capacity, as high as possible.
  • Fan out in batches, and keep big posts in a queue of their own, or one celebrity delays everyone.
  • Feed lists hold ids, are capped, live in memory for active users only, and can be rebuilt.
  • Rank in stages: a cheap model over all candidates, the full model over a few hundred.
  • Page a ranked feed from an order saved for the session; page a chronological one by id cursor.

Check yourself

Why do inserts per post and lists per read add up to about the same number under every strategy?

Each follow is paid for once. If the author is fanned out, the follow costs one insert per post; if not, one list read per feed request. A strategy only decides which side pays for each follow.

Feeds are read 50 times for every post. Which is cheaper, fan-out on write or on read, and by roughly how much in the simulation?

Fan-out on write: 112 operations for a post and its 50 reads, against 3,052 for fan-out on read, 27 times as many. A write is paid once, a read 50 times.

Why not exempt every account with more than 1,000 followers, to be safe?

Each exemption moves that account’s followers to the read side. On the simulated network, a threshold of 1,000 makes the average feed read merge 24 lists instead of 1, and the 99th percentile 103.

How would you choose the celebrity threshold?

From the delivery target and the workers’ spare capacity: an account whose fan-out cannot finish within the target even with all spare capacity should be exempt. With 50,000 spare entries a second and 5 seconds, that is 250,000 followers.

A post from an account with 2 million followers enters the same queue as everyone else, split into batches. What happens to ordinary posts?

They wait behind the 2,000 batches. In the simulation, ordinary posts published in the next minute took up to 25 seconds to deliver, against under half a second when big posts had their own queue served after small ones.

Why save the ranked order for the session instead of ranking again for each page?

Scores change between pages, so ranking again shows some posts twice and skips others. In the test, re-ranking half an hour later repeated about 4 posts and skipped about 3; the saved order repeated none.

A feed cache partition loses all its copies. What do its readers see, and how does the system recover?

A slower first page. The feed service rebuilds each reader’s list once by fan-out on read, merging the timelines of everyone they follow, and stores it again. No post is lost, because the lists only hold ids of posts stored elsewhere.
