Why a method

introduced a loop of six steps for any design. This chapter turns it into the procedure every case study in Part XI follows, with the interface and the data as two steps, because the second depends on the first. A high-level design problem names a product, “design a URL shortener”, and gives you 45 to 60 minutes. What is assessed is how you get from a vague sentence to a defensible architecture. Without a method, three things go wrong: boxes are drawn before anyone knows what they are for, one part takes half the time, and the failures are never reached.

The same list describes a good design review at work. The interview compresses it into an hour.

The seven steps

The order matters more than the exact minutes, because each step produces what the next one needs: numbers before estimates, estimates before boxes, an API before the data it reads.

StepYou produceInterviewers listen for
1. RequirementsThree to five features, a list of what is out of scope, and a number for each quality: scale, latency, availability, durability, consistencyQuestions whose answers change the design. Numbers instead of adjectives
2. EstimatesRequests a second at average and peak, storage, bandwidth and memory, rounded, each with what it decidesQuick arithmetic said aloud, followed by “so …”
3. The APIThe few operations, with requests, responses and errorsSafe retries for writes, pagination for lists, errors that match the requirements
4. The data modelThe entities, their keys, and a store for each, chosen by how it is readA key chosen for the main read. A reason for each store, not a brand
5. The designAn architecture drawing, with the main write and read traced through itThe simplest design first. Every box justified by a requirement or a number
6. Deep divesTwo or three hard parts, each with options, a choice and its costKnowing which parts are hard. Trade-offs with numbers
7. Failures and growthWhat breaks and what users see; what changes at 10 and 100 times the loadNamed failures and their recovery, not “it is highly available”

Every case study that follows uses these seven headings, so “Step 4” means the same thing in each. A case study opens with the problem as an interviewer would state it. After step 7 it adds what named real systems do, the follow-up questions an interviewer is likely to ask, and the common mistakes. Its code is small simulations that make the deep dives’ claims checkable.

A time plan

About 40 minutes of a 45-minute round are yours once introductions and your own questions are taken out. Two checkpoints matter. If the architecture is not on the board with one request traced through it by minute 27, or 32 in the longer round, stop refining earlier steps and draw it. And leave time for step 7: a design whose failures were never discussed is judged as if it had none planned.

Published frameworks agree on the shape. Hello Interview’s suggests about 5 minutes for requirements, 2 for the core entities, 5 for the API, 10 to 15 for the high-level design and about 10 for deep dives, and it treats estimates as optional: do the arithmetic only where it will change the design. Alex Xu’s System Design Interview allows a few minutes to understand the problem, 10 to 15 for the high-level design, 10 to 25 for the deep dive and a few to wrap up.

In an interview. Say the plan in the first minute: “I’ll agree the requirements and rough numbers, sketch the API and data, draw a first design, then go deep on the two hardest parts and finish with failures.” Then check in at each step. A question from the interviewer is the agenda: answer it, even if it reorders the plan.

The worked example: a pastebin

The problem. Design a pastebin. A user pastes some text and gets a short link to share. Anyone with the link can read the text until it expires. Pastes cannot be edited.

At the scale chosen here a pastebin is not hard, which is why it is the example: every step is visible without one difficult part taking over the chapter. The interesting decisions come from a latency target and from expiry, not from the load. Generating short keys is shared with , which treats it in depth.

Step 1: Requirements

Step 1 produces two short lists, written where the interviewer can see them. The functional list says what the system does and, as important, what it does not. The non-functional list gives each quality a number (). Spend about 5 minutes, 6 in a longer round. The questions worth asking are the ones whose answers change the design, and they fall into five groups.

Step 2: Estimates

Step 2 turns the requirements into the quantities of : traffic, storage, bandwidth, memory and machines. Round hard, say each line aloud, and spend about 3 minutes, 4 in a longer round. Interviewers listen less for the numbers than for what follows them.

creates 2,000,000 × 0.25 = 500,000 a day ÷ 86,400 ≈ 5.8 a second peak × 3 ≈ 17 reads 2,000,000 × 5 = 10,000,000 a day ÷ 86,400 ≈ 116 a second peak × 3 ≈ 350 10,000,000 ÷ 500,000 = 20 reads for each paste lifetime 0.1 × 1/24 + 0.3 × 1 + 0.35 × 7 + 0.2 × 30 + 0.05 × 365 ≈ 27 days on average alive 500,000 a day × 27 days = 13.5 million pastes, once a year has passed storage 13.5 million × 10 KB = 135 GB of text × 300 bytes = 4 GB of rows out 116 × 10 KB ≈ 1.2 MB/s ≈ 9.3 Mbit/s peak ≈ 28 Mbit/s memory one day of new pastes: 500,000 × 10 KB = 5 GB

The line for pastes alive uses : the number of things in a system is the rate at which they arrive times how long each stays. The 300 bytes for a row, with its share of the index, is an assumption to check later. Then say what the numbers decide.

Every quantity is under half of one ordinary server. The first design needs no partitioning: one database with a standby, a cache of about 5 GB for the latency target, and more than one app server for failures rather than for load.

The planning figures for one server are those of , rounded. They are crude, and enough for the question the drawing asks: is anything near the limit of one machine? At the defaults nothing is within a factor of ten. Try 200 million daily users to see which limits are crossed first.

Common mistake. Estimating and never using the result. Arithmetic that ends in “OK, it’s big” has decided nothing. End every estimate with a sentence that starts “so”: so one database is enough, so the cache must hold a day of pastes.

Step 3: The API

Step 3 lists the operations the outside world can call, with what each takes, returns and refuses. Spend about 3 minutes, 4 in a longer round. It exposes misunderstandings early: if you cannot say what creating a paste returns, you are not ready to draw servers. Interviewers listen for what makes an API safe to use: retried writes, paged lists, and the errors a caller must handle ().

  • Retries. A client that times out does not know whether its paste was created. The idempotency key lets it send the same request again and receive the first answer, not a second paste ().
  • Pagination. No operation returns a list. Adding “my recent pastes” would bring cursor pagination ().
  • Errors. 413 Content Too Large above 512 KB, 400 for an unknown expiry, 429 Too Many Requests when an address exceeds its limit, and 410 for a paste that existed and has gone ().
  • The id. Eight random characters from the 62 letters and digits give 628, about 2.2 × 1014, possible links. They are unguessable, which matters because the link is the only thing protecting a paste (). With 13.5 million alive, a new id collides with one in about 16 million attempts, and the database’s primary key catches it so the server can draw again.

Step 4: The data model

Step 4 names the entities, their keys and a store for each, chosen by how each is read. Spend about 3 minutes, 4 in a longer round. Interviewers listen for a key that serves the main read and a reason for every store. “I’ll use Cassandra” is not a reason; “every read is by id, and nothing joins” is ().

Keeping the text in the database was the alternative. At 135 GB it would work, with one store and one network hop fewer. The text goes to object storage because it never changes, because lifecycle rules can delete it with no code of ours, and because a database of 4 GB backs up and fails over in a fraction of the time one of 140 GB takes (). The price is latency: reading from object storage is slower than reading a row, and step 6 has to pay that back. Naming the rejected option and its price is what turns a choice into a design.

Step 5: The design

Step 5 produces the architecture drawing and the main requests traced through it. Spend about 10 minutes, 11 in a longer round. Start with the simplest arrangement that meets the functional requirements, and add a box only when a requirement or a number from step 2 asks for it, saying which. Interviewers listen for that reason, and for a design complete from end to end before any part of it is elaborate ().

The estimates said one database is enough, so the drawing has one, with a synchronous standby for durability (). Several app servers are there for availability, not load: one would carry 350 reads a second, but its loss would stop everything. Then trace the requests that matter. Tracing is where gaps appear: a component drawn but never called, or a step that needs data nobody has.

Two details of the create path deserve a sentence each. The text is stored before the row, so a failure between the two leaves an object nothing points to, which the lifecycle rule removes, rather than a row whose text is missing. And the cache is filled at creation, for a reason step 6 measures.

Step 6: Deep dives

A deep dive is a part of a design that is hard for the problem at hand, worked out properly: the options, a choice, and what the choice costs. Step 6 takes two or three. It gets the largest block of time, about 11 minutes, 16 in a longer round, and most of the assessment. Choosing the parts is half of it. Good candidates are, in order:

  • A number that breaks the simple design. A write rate one database cannot take, a data set one machine cannot hold, a fan-out of thousands for each request.
  • A requirement the simple design may not meet. A latency percentile, a durability promise, a rule such as “never sell the same seat twice”.
  • What makes this problem different from the last one. Its shape, in the table later in this chapter.
  • Whatever the interviewer asks about. A question is a request for a deep dive, and it outranks the list above.

Avoid parts that are hard in general but not here, such as the load balancer. For the pastebin the first item yields nothing, because the load breaks nothing. The second yields the 100 ms target, and the third yields expiry.

Deep dive: reading within 100 ms

A read that misses the cache pays for a row and an object storage read, and AWS gives roughly 100 to 200 ms for a small object in S3. Every miss can break the target, so it holds only if fewer than one read in a hundred misses. How large must the cache be? Step 2 cannot say, because the answer depends on when pastes are read, not only how often.

The chapter’s code answers it by simulation, with stated assumptions. Pastes arrive at a tenth of the real rate, with cache sizes scaled to match. Each gets a number of reads from a heavy-tailed distribution with an average of 20: the median paste is read 3 times, 20% are never read. Reads come an average of six hours after creation. Six days are replayed through a least-recently-used cache (), counting hits over the last four. One pass gives every size at once: a read hits exactly when its reuse distance, the number of different pastes touched since its own last use, is less than the number the cache holds, the stack-distance method of Mattson and colleagues (1970). A test checks this against replaying each size.

Two results decide the design. Filling the cache only on a miss can never pass 96%: 4% of reads are the first read of their paste, and a first read always misses. Filling at creation as well removes those misses, and then 5 GB, one day of new pastes, answers 99.47% of reads. At peak, the stores then see 1.8 reads a second instead of 14.4. The cost is memory spent on the 20% of pastes nobody reads. The assumption that matters most is how soon reads follow creation: if a day later on average instead of six hours, 5 GB answers 89% and passing 99% takes 20 GB. That is the number to measure first in production.

Deep dive: expiry

Expiry decides how much is stored, and the estimate of 13.5 million pastes alive hides a trap: Little’s law gives the level the count settles at, not how long it takes to get there. The simulation creates pastes with the expiry mix of step 1 and counts those alive each day.

A month after launch 5.1 million pastes are alive, 38% of the eventual 13.5 million, and the count climbs until the first one-year pastes expire. Those are 5% of the pastes created but two thirds of what is stored. A plan drawn from the first month would be too small by a factor of 2.6. Two choices follow.

  • Expiry is checked on every read. The row’s expires_at is compared with the time, and an expired paste gets 410 whether or not it has been deleted yet. Correctness then never depends on the cleanup.
  • Cleanup is batch work that must keep up. Once the count is level, 500,000 pastes expire a day, about 6 a second. A sweeper deletes expired rows in batches through the index on expires_at, and lifecycle rules on each expiry prefix delete the text. Amazon S3’s rules count in whole days and run asynchronously, so a one-hour paste’s text lingers a day or more, unreadable because its row has expired. At a hundred times the load, a table partitioned by expiry date, with whole partitions dropped, replaces row-by-row deletes ().

Step 7: Failures and growth

Step 7 goes through the design box by box and asks what happens when each is slow or gone, then what changes when the load grows. Spend the last 4 minutes, 7 in a longer round. “It is highly available” is not an answer; a named failure with its recovery is ().

That is easy because every read is by id and nothing joins (). The text in object storage needs no change at any of these sizes. Saying what does not change is half of the answer.

The same method in a design review

At work, the seven steps become the sections of a design document, a written proposal for a system that others review before the building starts, asking the interviewer’s questions at leisure. The conditions differ in the ways lists. The order of the thinking does not.

StepSection of the documentWhat reviewers check
1. RequirementsContext, goals and non-goalsIs every quality a number? Is what is out written down?
2. EstimatesCapacityAre the assumptions stated, and which were measured?
3. The APIInterfacesErrors, retries, versions, and who calls it
4. The data modelDataKeys, stores, and the migration from what exists now
5. The designThe design, with diagramsA reason for every component. The main flows
6. Deep divesAlternatives consideredThe options rejected, and why
7. Failures and growthOperations, rollout, risksWhat alerts, what the on-call engineer does, how it is rolled back

Malte Ubl’s description of design documents at Google (2020) gives a similar outline: context and scope, goals and non-goals, the design with its diagrams, APIs and data storage, alternatives considered, and cross-cutting concerns such as security and privacy. It suggests 10 to 20 pages for a large project, and skipping the document when there is no real trade-off to decide. A single decision fits the one or two pages Michael Nygard proposed in 2011, an architecture decision record: title, context, decision, status and consequences.

Numbers for every requirement and a reason for every box carry over from the interview. The time box does not: a review that finds a flaw in the data model sends the design back to step 4. And at work the deep dives are chosen by risk. The parts hardest to change later, the data model and any public API, deserve the most review.

Key points

  • Work in seven steps: requirements, estimates, the API, the data model, the design, deep dives, failures and growth. Each produces what the next needs.
  • In 45 minutes, have the architecture drawn with one request traced through it by about minute 27, and keep the last 4 minutes for failures.
  • Ask the questions whose answers change the design, and write every quality as a number.
  • End every estimate with “so”, and the decision it settles.
  • Give every store a reason based on how it is read, and name the option you rejected.
  • Choose deep dives where a number breaks the simple design, where a requirement is at risk, or where the problem’s shape lies.
  • Some answers need a model, not arithmetic: the pastebin’s cache must be filled at creation to pass 99%, and its storage grows for a whole year.
  • For each component, say what fails, what users see and what the design does; then say what changes, and what does not, at 10 and 100 times the load.
  • At work the same steps become a design document, with the deep dives written as alternatives considered.

Check yourself

An interviewer says “design a photo-sharing app” and you begin by drawing a load balancer and three app servers. What have you skipped, and what does it cost?

Steps 1 to 4. Without requirements no number justifies any box, and without an API and data model the boxes have nothing to do. The likely cost is a design for the wrong problem and no time left for the deep dives.

Your estimate gives 17 writes a second at peak. What do you say next?

What it decides: “so one database takes the writes with plenty of room, and there is no reason to partition.”

Why can a cache that is filled only on a miss never answer more than about 96% of the pastebin’s reads, however large it is?

Because 4% of the reads in the simulation are the first read of their paste, and a cache filled on a miss holds nothing that has not been read once. Filling it at creation removes those misses, and takes the hit ratio past 99% with 5 GB.

Little’s law says 13.5 million pastes will be alive. You measure 5 million after the first month and plan storage for 6 million. What is wrong?

The law gives the level the count settles at, reached only after the longest lifetime, a year. In the simulation the count is 5.1 million after a month and 13.5 million after a year, because one-year pastes keep accumulating.

Twenty-eight minutes into a 45-minute round you are still refining the data model. What do you do?

Stop, draw the architecture with what you have, and trace one request through it. A complete simple design with a deep dive scores better than a perfect data model with no system around it.

For a ticket-selling system, which of these is the better deep dive: the load balancer’s algorithm, or what happens when 50,000 people try to buy the last 100 seats?

The second. It is the problem’s shape, contended inventory, and it puts the central requirement, never selling a seat twice, at risk. Any reasonable load balancer would work.

At a hundred times the users, what changes in the pastebin, and what does not?

Peak creates pass about 1,000 writes a second and the rows reach 405 GB, so the table is partitioned by a hash of the id, and the cache becomes a cluster of about 500 GB. The object storage, the API and the read path’s logic do not change.
