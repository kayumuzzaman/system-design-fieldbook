The problem

The problem. Design a video platform like YouTube. Creators upload videos from a browser or a phone. Viewers watch them on phones, laptops and televisions, on fast and slow connections, and can skip to any point. Playback should start quickly and should not freeze. The most popular videos are watched by millions of people at the same time.

Two things make this hard, and neither is the application logic. First, the bytes: every upload becomes a dozen versions, and viewers receive hundreds of bytes for every byte creators send. Second, the network to each viewer is outside the platform’s control and changes by the second, so the player must keep choosing what to fetch. After a short account of how video is stored and played, the method of takes them in order.

Video in four ideas

Raw video is far too large to send: 1920 × 1080 pixels of 3 bytes each, 30 times a second, is about 1.5 Gbit/s. A codec is the method that compresses video into a bitstream and decompresses it again for display; H.264 (2003), HEVC (2013) and AV1 (2018) are the common ones, each newer one needing fewer bits for the same quality and more computation to encode. Most frames are stored as differences from the frames around them. A keyframe is a frame stored whole, which can be decoded without any other frame, so playback can start only at a keyframe.

Transcoding is decoding a video and encoding it again at another resolution, bitrate or codec. A platform transcodes every upload into a set of versions, and that set, ordered from smallest to largest, is the bitrate ladder; each version is a rung. Apple’s guidelines for its devices give an example ladder for H.264 with nine rungs, from 416×234 at 145 kbit/s to 1920×1080 at 7,800 kbit/s, which this chapter uses throughout.

Then each rung is cut into short segments, every one starting with a keyframe, at the same instants in every rung; Apple recommends 6 seconds, the length used here. Adaptive bitrate streaming is playing a video as a sequence of such segments, with the player choosing for each segment which rung to fetch, according to how its connection is doing. The segments are ordinary files fetched with HTTP GET, so any web server or CDN can deliver them ().

Two formats describe the segments to the player. HTTP Live Streaming (HLS) is Apple’s format, published as RFC 8216 in 2017: a master playlist lists the rungs, and a media playlist for each rung lists its segments. MPEG-DASH (Dynamic Adaptive Streaming over HTTP) is the international standard ISO/IEC 23009-1, first published in 2012, which describes the same structure in an XML manifest. Since 2018 a common segment format, CMAF, lets one set of segment files serve both, so a platform stores its segments once and writes two small manifests.

Step 5: The design

Only small requests reach the application: creating an upload, reading a video’s record, posting events. Everything heavy goes around it. Uploads go to object storage with signed URLs, processing runs as queued work on its own pool, and playback is served by the CDN from files that never change.

Two details carry the requirements. The workflow publishes the video once the lowest rungs exist, meeting the “public within a few minutes” target while higher rungs are still encoding. And the player starts low because it knows nothing yet about its connection: a 360p segment is 274 KB, which arrives in a fraction of a second on almost any connection.

Step 6a: Transcoding in parallel

Processing an upload means decoding the original, encoding it nine times, cutting each rung into segments and writing the playlists. The chapter’s code models the cost: each rung costs core-seconds in proportion to its pixels, 3.2 for each second of 1080p, plus 0.3 for decoding, varying with the complexity of each scene. The ladder costs 14.3 core-seconds per second of video, the figure in step 2, so a 10-minute upload is 2.3 core-hours of work.

Giving each rung its own worker stops helping at nine workers, because the 1080p rungs alone take over half an hour. The better plan cuts the original at keyframes into chunks, encodes each chunk of each rung as a separate job, and joins the results: a map step and a reduce step (). Each job pays a fixed 4 seconds in the model to start, fetch its piece and write its output. The simulation schedules the jobs on a pool of workers, largest first.

Each plan has a floor: the longest single job. Smaller chunks lower the floor, a minute for 6-second chunks against 34 for whole files, but every job pays its overhead again, so 6-second chunks do 43% more work than whole files. Chunks of 30 to 60 seconds get the time under five minutes for 4 to 9% more work. Short chunks have a second cost that the model leaves out: an encoder that sees only a few seconds plans its bits with less to go on.

The order of the queue decides when the video can go public. With 30-second chunks on 32 workers, largest first finishes everything in 5.1 minutes but completes no rung until near the end. Lowest rungs first has three rungs ready after 1.2 minutes, so the video goes public then, and finishes in 6.1.

The steps run as a durable workflow: split, encode, assemble, publish (). A worker that dies makes its job reappear in the queue (), and every output’s name is fixed by video, rung and chunk, so a job that runs twice writes the same files. The pool of step 2, 30,000 cores, depends more on the codec and the hardware than on any scheduling. Nor must every video get the same ladder. Per-title encoding is choosing the rungs of each video from an analysis of that video, since a cartoon needs far fewer bits than a football match for the same quality.

Step 6b: Choosing a bitrate, segment by segment

The player holds a buffer of downloaded video, up to 60 seconds in the model, and chooses a rung before fetching each segment. Too high, and the segment takes longer to arrive than the buffer lasts, so the picture freezes. Too low, and the picture is worse than the connection allows. Players use two signals.

  • Throughput-based. Estimate the bandwidth from how fast recent segments arrived, and pick the highest rung under a fraction of it. The browser player hls.js documents its version: the smaller of two moving averages with half-lives of 3 and 9 seconds, and a rung must fit in 95% of it, or 70% to switch up.
  • Buffer-based. Let the buffer decide, as in the code below, which moves one rung at a time unless the line has passed several. Huang and colleagues at Stanford and Netflix proposed this in 2014, finding that estimates of a varying connection are either wrong or too cautious, while the buffer already holds the answer: it grows when the rung is below the bandwidth and shrinks when above.
export class BufferBased implements Abr {
  readonly name: string;
  private readonly reservoir: number;
  private readonly cushion: number;
  constructor(reservoir = 24, cushion = 30) {
    this.reservoir = reservoir;
    this.cushion = cushion;
    this.name = `buffer-based, reservoir ${reservoir} s`;
  }
  choose(view: View): number {
    const top = LADDER.length - 1, b = view.buffer;
    if (b <= this.reservoir) return 0;
    if (b >= this.reservoir + this.cushion) return top;
    const lo = (LADDER[0] as Rung).kbps, hi = (LADDER[top] as Rung).kbps;
    const f = lo + (hi - lo) * (b - this.reservoir) / this.cushion;
    const prev = Math.max(view.last, 0);
    const up = Math.min(prev + 1, top), down = Math.max(prev - 1, 0);
    if (f >= (LADDER[up] as Rung).kbps) return Math.max(highestBelow(f), prev);
    if (f <= (LADDER[down] as Rung).kbps) {
      for (let i = 0; i <= top; i++) if ((LADDER[i] as Rung).kbps > f) return Math.min(i, prev);
    }
    return prev;
  }
}

Each throughput setting buys bitrate with frozen time along one curve, and the buffer-based players sit above it: at the same frozen time, interpolated, the 24-second reservoir delivered 1.28 times the bitrate and the 36-second one 1.56 times. A throughput player with a full buffer and a cautious estimate pays twice for the same safety.

The buffer-based player has two costs. It changes rung more often, 25 times a session against 16, which viewers can see. And it starts at the bottom: its first minute averaged 1,339 kbit/s. The paper’s later versions sped up the start with how fast the first segments arrived; the last row follows the throughput estimate until the buffer map catches up, for a first minute at 3,049 kbit/s and a little more freezing. dash.js, the DASH Industry Forum’s player, switches by buffer level between a throughput rule and a buffer rule called BOLA (Spiteri, Urgaonkar and Sitaraman, 2016).

Most remaining stalls came from a drop while a large segment was on its way. Real players abandon such a download and fetch the segment from a lower rung, as a dash.js rule does; the model does not, so its stalls are an upper bound.

Step 6c: Delivery from the edge

At 33 Tbit/s, delivery is a cache problem with three facts working against it. Popularity has a long tail: a few videos get most views, and millions get a handful each. Viewers leave early, so a video’s first segments are requested far more than its last. And an edge can serve only what it has fetched before, so each segment’s first request at each location is a miss ().

The simulation sends 100,000 views of 5,000 videos, chosen with Zipf’s law (exponent 0.9), to 4 edges. Viewers use three rungs, 6,000, 3,000 and 1,100 kbit/s, and leave after 30 segments on average. Each edge’s cache is sized as a share of the 3.2 TB catalog, in three arrangements: caching on demand, evicting the least recently used segment (); the same with a shield four times as large behind (); and filled in advance with the segments a forecast expects most, the forecast of each video’s popularity being off by a random factor of typically two.

With edges holding 5% of the catalog, they served 51% of the bytes alone and 75% with the shield, so the shield cut what the origin sends from 49% to 25%. Edges caching on demand gain slowly beyond that, reaching 69% at the largest size, because most of what they miss are first requests. A shield is shared, so the same segment’s first request at each of the four edges reaches the origin once. The forecast fill avoids first requests altogether and beat on-demand caching at every size, 78% against 69% at the largest, even with a forecast that is often wrong by a factor of two. Its weakness is not in the model, whose popularity does not change during the day: a video that becomes popular at noon is not in a cache filled at night. So a fill is combined with caching on demand.

Data leaving a cloud is priced by the gigabyte (), so at this scale each point of hit ratio is worth more than any server in the design. That is why the largest platforms run their own CDNs, with servers inside their viewers’ ISPs.

Common mistake. Letting the signature of a signed URL into the cache key. Every viewer’s URL is different, so every request becomes a different object and the hit ratio falls to almost nothing. The edge checks the signature and then caches by the path alone ().

In the real world

  • The formats. HLS is RFC 8216 (August 2017), an informational RFC. Its second edition, draft-pantos-hls-rfc8216bis, was at revision 22 in May 2026 and had been sent to the RFC Editor but not published when checked in October 2026. MPEG-DASH is ISO/IEC 23009-1, first published in April 2012; its sixth edition appeared in July 2026. RFC 8216 requires that matching content in every variant have matching timestamps, so that clients can switch, and that a live client start at least three target durations from the end of the playlist.
  • Apple’s rules for its devices (checked October 2026): video in H.264, HEVC, Dolby Vision or AV1; a keyframe every 2 seconds and at the start of every segment; 6-second segments. This chapter’s ladder is its example for H.264.
  • Netflix. In March 2016 it reported over 125 million hours of viewing a day, peaks of tens of terabits a second, and Open Connect servers in close to 1,000 locations, many inside ISPs, which its overview says are filled off-peak from predictions of what members will watch. Per-title encoding (2015) gave one title’s top 1080p stream 4,640 kbit/s instead of the fixed ladder’s 5,800. In December 2025 AV1 carried about 30% of its viewing, with a third less bandwidth than H.264 and HEVC and 45% fewer buffering interruptions. Its encoding service (2024) splits, encodes chunks in parallel and assembles.
  • YouTube says more than 20 million videos are uploaded on an average day (press page, checked October 2026), forty times this chapter’s assumption. In 2021 Google described its video coding unit, a custom chip for transcoding deployed in its data centers, with 20 to 33 times the efficiency of its earlier, well-tuned software encoding.
  • Players. In A/B tests in 2013 with over half a million Netflix users each, the buffer-based approach reduced the rebuffer rate by 10 to 20% against the default algorithm of the time, at a similar average rate.

Key points

  • Video is many versions of every upload: a ladder of rungs, each cut into short segments that start with keyframes at the same instants.
  • Viewers receive hundreds of bytes for every byte uploaded, so delivery from a CDN is the design, and the application servers only sign URLs and keep records.
  • Uploads go straight to object storage in parts; processing is a durable workflow of jobs that can run twice safely.
  • Encode chunks in parallel, not whole files: time falls from 34 minutes to a few, for a few percent more work, and the lowest rungs first make the video public early.
  • The player chooses a rung for every segment. Letting the buffer level decide gave 1.3 to 1.6 times the bitrate of a throughput-based player at the same frozen time in the simulation.
  • Edges caching on demand miss every first request; a shared shield halves what reaches the origin, and a fill from a forecast avoids the first misses.

Check yourself

Why must every rung be cut into segments at the same instants, each starting with a keyframe?

A player changes rung between segments. If segment 12 starts at the same instant in every rung and needs no earlier frame, the player can take segment 11 from one rung and 12 from another, or start at 12 after a seek, without a glitch.

Why does the upload API hand out signed URLs instead of receiving the file?

So that the gigabytes go straight to object storage. The API only authorizes and records the upload, no application server is tied up carrying bytes, and a dropped connection costs one part.

A 10-minute video takes 34 minutes to encode with one worker per rung, even with 500 workers. Why, and what fixes it?

There are only nine jobs, and the 1080p rung alone takes 34 minutes. Chunks cut at keyframes make hundreds of smaller jobs: 2.5 minutes on 128 workers with 30-second chunks in the simulation.

Why not cut chunks as small as possible?

Every job pays a fixed overhead to start, fetch its piece and write its output: in the model, 6-second chunks did 43% more work than whole files.

A throughput-based player keeps its buffer full yet delivers a lower bitrate than a buffer-based one with the same frozen time. Why?

It pays twice for safety: a cautious estimate, and a full buffer it never draws on. A buffer-based player uses the buffer itself as the margin.

Why does a shield behind the edges cut origin traffic so much?

Each edge misses the first request it gets for a segment, so without a shield the origin sends it once per edge. The shared shield fetches it once and answers the other edges’ misses; in the simulation it halved what the origin sent.

What goes wrong if the signature of a signed URL is part of the CDN’s cache key?

Every viewer’s URL differs, so every request is a different object and almost nothing is a hit. The edge should check the signature and cache by the path alone.
